> **In one line:** a good coding-interview plan is **topics in order, practised right after you study them, then mixed practice, with mock interviews started once you are about 60% through**. The Tech Interview Handbook suggests about 3 months at about 11 hours a week. This lesson turns that plan into a 12-week table that points to the matching lessons on this site.

Most people fail to prepare in one of two ways. They grind random problems with no order, or they read about topics and never solve anything under time pressure. A plan fixes both. This lesson explains one public plan, shows how many hours it asks for, and maps each week to the lessons of the [DSA series](/dsa) so that you learn and practise in one place.

## Words used in this lesson

- **Breadth-first** means covering every topic quickly, then practising across all of them.
- **Depth-first** means studying one topic until you are good at it, then moving to the next.
- **Pattern** means a reusable way to solve a family of problems (two pointers, sliding window, BFS).
- **Corner case** (or edge case) means an unusual input that breaks naive code: empty input, one element, duplicates, huge values.
- **Mock interview** means a practice interview with another person who plays the interviewer.
- **Grind 75** is a question list and planning tool made by the handbook's author. You choose the number of weeks and hours and it generates a plan.

## The numbers from the handbook

The handbook gives these figures. Use them to check your own plan, not to copy it:

| What | The handbook says |
|---|---|
| A coding interview round | 30 to 45 minutes |
| Bare minimum preparation | about 30 hours |
| Well prepared | about 100 hours |
| Recommended holistic plan | about 3 months, at about 11 hours a week (about 2 to 3 hours a day) |
| A naive pace | 30 minutes per question and 3 hours a day, about 160 questions in 3 to 4 weeks |
| Self-introduction and "questions for the interviewer" | about 3 hours |
| Start mock interviews | when you are about 60% through the plan |

The handbook warns that the naive pace "may not build the right approach or retention", and that aimlessly grinding questions is a poor use of time. It also says LeetCode alone is not enough. It advises keeping your time estimates conservative so that you do not burn out.

### Three ways to order your study

The handbook describes three approaches:

1. **Breadth-first.** Revise every topic, then practise across all topics. Suggested if you have about a month.
2. **Depth-first.** Study and practise one topic at a time until you have mastered it. Suggested if time is short; focus on the high-priority topics.
3. **Depth-first, then breadth.** Go topic by topic with a few questions each, then do a mixed practice phase. Suggested for more than a month.

The author recommends the first or the third. The reason given is that some breadth-level practice is needed so that you do not forget the earlier topics.

## The handbook's 12-week shape

**Weeks 1 to 4: study each topic, then practise it straight away.** The topics, with the handbook's priority and time:

| Week | Topics (priority, hours) | Total |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Array (high, 2), String (high, 3), Hash table (mid, 3), Recursion (mid, 3) | 11 h |
| 2 | Sorting and searching (high, 3), Matrix (high, 1), Linked list (mid, 3), Queue (mid, 2), Stack (mid, 2) | 11 h |
| 3 | Tree (high, 4), Graph (high, 4), Heap (mid, 3), Trie (mid, 3) | 14 h |
| 4 | Interval (mid, 2), Dynamic programming (low, 4), Binary (low, 2), Math (low, 1), Geometry (low, 1) | 10 h |

**Weeks 5 to 12: in-depth practice.** The handbook lists 75 questions, generated with its Grind 75 tool. You may skip or repeat questions from weeks 1 to 4. It says dynamic programming questions can be skipped if you did not study the topic or it seems irrelevant, because many can be solved with recursion or backtracking. It recommends tracking progress in the tool.

**In parallel:** write your self-introduction and your final questions (about 3 hours), and **start mock interviews at about 60% of the plan**, which in a 12-week plan is around week 7.

## How to practise a topic

The handbook's advice is to study a single concept and practise relevant questions straight away, using its cheatsheets next to your practice. The rest of this section is my own routine for doing that well:

1. **Learn the idea first.** Read the lesson, trace the example by hand on paper, then close it.
2. **Solve a question cold, with a timer.** Give yourself about 30 to 40 minutes (the handbook assumes 30 minutes per question for its pace). Say your thinking out loud as in a real interview.
3. **When you are stuck, use the order:** a smaller example, then a hint, then the solution. Write down what hint you needed.
4. **Read a good solution after you finish**, even if yours worked. Compare time and space cost.
5. **Write a one-line pattern note.** For example: "sorted array and pair sum: two pointers". Patterns, not memorised answers, are what carry over.
6. **Re-solve the same question after a few days without looking.** (Spaced review is my suggestion, not the handbook's, but it is easy to test on yourself: if you cannot solve it again, you had not learned it.)

## A weekly routine

| Day | What |
|---|---|
| Mon, Tue | Study the week's topic and solve 3 to 4 questions on it |
| Wed | Solve 2 questions from last week's topic without notes (review) |
| Thu | Mixed set: 3 questions from different topics, timed |
| Fri | Read your mistake log ([Lesson 2](/career/lesson-2)) and re-solve 2 old mistakes |
| Weekend | One longer session: a mock interview (from week 7) or 2 timed questions plus a written review |

Adjust the days to your life. The point is: new material, review, mixed practice and a look at your mistakes **every week**.

## Choosing a language

The handbook recommends a language you know well and that suits interviews, and names Python, C++, Java and JavaScript as common choices. This site's DSA series uses JavaScript. If you already work in JavaScript or TypeScript, use it, and learn its sharp edges: sorting numbers with `.sort()` sorts them as text unless you pass a compare function, and there is no built-in heap or ordered map, so know how you would write or avoid them. (Check with your target company that your language is allowed.)

## Interview day, short version

- Sleep and eat. Arrive early, or test your video, audio and editor setup 30 minutes before a remote interview.
- Say your plan before you code: restate the problem, ask about inputs and sizes, give two examples, outline the approach, say the expected time and space cost.
- Start with a simple correct solution if you cannot see the best one, then improve it.
- Test with your own examples, including empty and one-element inputs, before you say "done".
- Ask your final questions. Prepare them in advance (the handbook's estimate for the introduction and final questions together is about 3 hours).

The techniques inside the interview are the subject of [Lesson 2](/career/lesson-2).

## Key takeaways

- A plan beats random grinding: topics in order, practise right after studying, then mixed practice, then mocks.
- The handbook's figures: about 30 hours at the minimum, about 100 hours to be well prepared, and a 3-month plan at about 11 hours a week.
- Three orders: breadth-first, depth-first, or depth-first then breadth (recommended with more than a month).
- Learn patterns, not answers. Write a pattern note after each question.
- Review old mistakes every week. Spaced review is an easy way to test what you really learned.
- Start mock interviews at about 60% of the plan, and keep time estimates conservative.

> **In one line:** a software-engineer resume is a **one-page, plain, scannable summary of what you changed and how much it mattered**. It has standard headings, specific bullets with real numbers, and the keywords of the job you want. It does not try to look clever.

Most people write a resume once, list their duties, and then wonder why nobody replies. This lesson shows a simple way to build one, based on one well-known public guide. It covers what to put in, what to cut, what is myth about applicant tracking systems (ATS), and how to rewrite weak bullets. It ends with a checklist.

## Words used in this lesson

- **Resume**: a short document listing your work, skills and education. In the US it is the normal word; some countries say CV (curriculum vitae) for the same thing.
- **ATS (applicant tracking system)**: software a company uses to collect applications, parse resumes into fields and let recruiters search and sort them.
- **Parsing**: the software reading your file and splitting it into fields such as name, job titles and dates.
- **Recruiter**: a person who finds and screens candidates before the engineers talk to them.
- **Bullet**: one line item under a job, describing one thing you did.
- **Action verb**: a strong first word for a bullet, such as "built", "cut", "migrated", "led".
- **Scope**: how big the thing was: users, requests, team size, data size, money.
- **Keyword**: a word from the job post, such as "React" or "PostgreSQL", that a person or a search tool looks for.
- **Metric**: a number that measures a result, such as "p95 latency down from 800 ms to 300 ms".

## Who reads your resume, and for how long

Two kinds of readers see your resume, often in this order:

1. **Software** (the ATS) stores it and may help the recruiter search it.
2. **A person** (a recruiter, then an engineer or hiring manager) scans it, usually quickly.

The guide says the person is "quickly scanning for keywords of skills or experiences that they value" because they have little time for each resume. It does **not** give a number of seconds. You will often hear "six seconds" or "30 seconds" online; I did not find a number in the guide, so I treat it as folklore. What matters is the point behind it: **assume a short, distracted read.**

So design for a scan. In the first screen of the page a reader should be able to answer four questions:

| Question | Where the answer lives |
| --- | --- |
| Who is this and where are they? | Name and contact line at the top |
| What kind of engineer, and how experienced? | Headline / summary |
| Do they have the skills we asked for? | Skills section, plus the first job's bullets |
| Did they do things that mattered? | Bullets with numbers |

My opinion: if a friend cannot tell you in ten seconds what you do and one result you are proud of, the page is not ready.

## One page

The guide says to use **only one page** and that "highlighting a few of your best achievements is better than including many average achievements". It also says not to list everything "just to showcase a greater quantity".

The reason is simple: every extra line makes the best lines harder to find. One page forces you to choose. If you have more than ten years of experience and one page truly cannot hold it, that is a judgement call; the guide does not discuss it, and I will not invent a rule.

## The structure

The guide recommends this order and these heading names ("recommended by recruiters", in the author's words):

| Order | Section | Heading to use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Professional summary | A one-line headline about you (not the words "Professional Summary") |
| 2 | Contact information | "Contact Information" |
| 3 | Skills | "Skills" |
| 4 | Experience | "Work Experience" |
| 5 | Education | "Education" |
| 6 | Projects | "Projects" |
| 7 | Optional | "Awards and Accolades" or "Certifications" |

Two notes from the guide: if you are still a student or have **under 3 years of experience**, you may put Education first. And **do not put symbols in headings**, because the guide says they can hurt ATS readability.

My opinion: many people put contact details in a single line directly under the name and skip a heading for it. That is fine for a human reader. The guide's table is about being safe with software.

### Summary (headline)

The guide says a summary helps because interviewers rarely have time to read the details. Its rules:

- Fewer than **50 words**.
- Answer "why am I a good fit for this job?"
- Active voice, action words, and start with the noun for your role ("Software Engineer", "Front End Engineer").
- Give it a **headline of fewer than 10 words** instead of the title "Professional Summary".

Its own examples include "Front End Engineer with X years of experience and strong fundamentals in Front End technologies" and, for a student, "Senior Year student at University X with a focus on Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning". Replace X with your real number.

A made-up example of mine, in that style:

> **Backend Engineer, 4 years, payments and APIs.** Backend engineer with 4 years of experience building REST APIs in Node.js and PostgreSQL. Reduced checkout errors and led a team of three on a billing migration.

### Contact information

The guide's must-haves: name at the very top, personal phone number, city/state/zip (enough for a recruiter to tell if you are local or international), personal email, and a LinkedIn profile. It says never to use a work phone or work email. Good to have: GitHub, personal site, and coding-platform profiles (with rankings or badges if impressive).

My opinion: if you include a link, check it opens while logged out. A dead link is worse than none.

### Skills

The guide gives this shape: `[Skill summary] : [list separated by "|"]`, grouped as programming languages, frameworks and databases. It also says that if your familiarity is impressive you can add the amount of code you have written ("Over 10,000 lines").

```text
Languages: JavaScript | TypeScript | Python | SQL
Frameworks: React | Next.js | Express
Databases: PostgreSQL | Redis
Tools: Git | Docker | GitHub Actions
```

Rules I use (my opinion): list only what you could discuss for ten minutes in an interview. A skills list is a promise the interviewer will test. Put the skills the job asks for first. Do not rate yourself with bars or stars: "4 out of 5 in Java" tells the reader nothing and cannot be parsed.

### Work experience

The guide's format for each job:

```text
[Company], [Location] | [Job Title] | [MM/YYYY - MM/YYYY]
```

Example from the guide: `Facebook, Singapore | Front End Engineering Lead | 08/2018 - Present`. List jobs in **reverse chronological order** (newest first). Under each job, give the **scope** of the job and its **top accomplishments**.

### Education

The guide's rules: unless you are a recent graduate, do not put education above work experience. Include a GPA only if it is **more than 3.50 / 4.00** (or above 4.3 on a 5-point scale). You can add leadership roles, societies, projects and awards.

### Projects

The guide says to include **at least 2 projects** with your key contribution, and to **link the project name to GitHub** or another place where the reader can look. Its example is a maintainer role on a documentation tool, with a clear statement of what the tool is and who uses it.

My opinion: a project is most useful when you have little work history. For each, say what it is in one sentence, what you built, the stack, and one number (users, stars, requests, test count). A link to a repo that has a README and a working demo beats a long description. If you want ideas for what to build and how to explain it, the [Full-Stack Lesson 14](/fullstack-qa/lesson-14) experience round shows how those projects get discussed later.

## Writing a good bullet

This is the part that changes results the most. A weak bullet says what your **job** was. A strong bullet says what **changed because of you**.

### The guide's formula

The guide says each accomplishment follows this shape:

> [Accomplishment summary] : [Action] that resulted in [quantifiable outcome]

So the three parts are: **what you achieved, what you did, and the measurable result**.

### About the "accomplished X as measured by Y by doing Z" idea

You may have seen a version of this sentence frame: "Accomplished X, as measured by Y, by doing Z." **I did not find that wording in the handbook guide.** I believe it comes from a different, widely shared recruiting tip, but I did not read that source, so I will not attribute it. The handbook's frame (summary, action, quantifiable outcome) carries the same idea, and I use it below.

### A practical bullet recipe (action + scope + result)

This is my way of using the guide's frame. Check each bullet for three things:

1. **Action**: starts with a strong verb in the past tense (present tense for your current job). "Built", "reduced", "migrated".
2. **Scope**: how big was it? Users, services, team size, data size, number of endpoints.
3. **Result**: what changed, as a number if you honestly have one: time saved, cost cut, errors reduced, revenue, adoption.

A bullet does not need all three every time. But a page full of bullets with none of the three is a job description, not a resume.

### Numbers you can defend

The guide asks for "quantifiable" results. The risk is a number you cannot explain. Interviewers read your bullets and ask about them (see [Full-Stack Lesson 14](/fullstack-qa/lesson-14)). A good rule from me:

- Use only numbers you can explain in a sentence: how it was measured, over what period, and compared to what.
- Prefer **before and after** ("from 12 minutes to 3 minutes") to a bare percentage.
- If you do not have a metric, use **scope** instead: "for a service handling about 2 million requests a day" is honest if you know the figure from a dashboard.
- Never round up. "About 30%" is fine when it was 28%. "Doubled" when it was 1.3 times is not.
- If you do not know a number and cannot find out, leave it out. A clear plain bullet is better than a doubtful number.

## Keywords and ATS: myths versus facts

Here I separate three things: what the guide **claims**, what I could **verify**, and my **opinion**. I could not verify anything about how a particular ATS works, because I did not have access to one.

| Statement | Status |
| --- | --- |
| "Many top companies use an ATS to collect and sort resumes." | The guide says so. Widely true in practice (my general knowledge), but I did not verify any specific company. |
| "The ATS may reject candidates automatically using rules." | The guide says "may even use certain rules to reject candidates automatically". **Not verified by me.** Treat it as possible, not certain. |
| "Use a PDF made from Word or Google Docs, with text you can highlight." | The guide's advice. A reason you can check yourself: if you can select the text, software can read it. |
| "Avoid headers and footers in the document; use narrow margins instead." | The guide's advice (margins of 0.5 inch). You can test by the plain-text test below. |
| "Use Arial, Calibri or Garamond, at least 10 pt." | The guide's advice. The reason it gives is that unusual fonts may turn into unreadable characters. |
| "Add symbols and icons to headings so you stand out." | The guide says **don't**: no symbols in headers. |
| "Some ATS rank you by how many times a keyword appears." | The guide says "some ATS" do this. **Not verified by me.** |
| "An ATS can add up your years of experience from where a keyword appears" (the guide's example: 3 years in a job could be counted as 5 years of a skill) | A claim in the guide, **not verified**. I would not build a strategy on it. |
| "Copy keywords from the job post." | The guide's advice, with a limit: copy the language for skills you truly have, and do not stuff keywords. |
| "Write the full name of abbreviations, such as Amazon Web Services as well as AWS." | The guide's advice; cheap and harmless. |
| "Online resume builders and design tools are fine." | The guide says **don't use** Photoshop, graphic tools or online resume builders. |

### What I would conclude (my opinion)

- The **format rules** (plain file, selectable text, standard headings, no tables-in-images) cost nothing and remove a real risk. Follow them.
- The **ranking and auto-reject claims** are unproven here. Do not twist your resume to chase them. A real person still reads the page in the end.
- The **keyword advice** is just good sense. If the post says "React" and "TypeScript" and you have used both, those words should appear on your page, in skills and in bullets.

### The plain-text test

The guide suggests a simple check: copy all the text from your resume and paste it into a plain text file. Edit your resume if:

- some points are missing,
- some characters show up wrongly, or
- the sections come out in the wrong order.

Do this every time you export. It takes one minute. It tests the thing that actually matters: can software read your file.

### Tailoring without lying

The guide suggests this for a group of similar jobs: collect 3 to 5 job posts for the role, paste them into one text file, and use a word-frequency tool to see which words repeat. Then make sure the skills and experience you **really have** show up in your resume. For one specific job, read the post for must-have and nice-to-have skills and mirror the language where it is true.

Two cautions from the guide: do not stuff keywords, and do not apply to many unrelated roles at the same company, because the ATS lets recruiters see all your applications. Also, if a company makes you retype your work history into its own form, take it seriously: the guide says the form data may be what is screened, and the recruiter may never open your file.

## What to cut

Using the guide's "less is more" rule plus my own judgement:

| Cut or shorten | Why |
| --- | --- |
| Duties with no result ("Responsible for the backend") | Says nothing about what changed |
| Old or unrelated jobs, or school jobs when you have real experience | Takes space from stronger lines |
| A long skills list with every tool you ever touched | Skills are promises that will be tested |
| Photo, date of birth, marital status | Not in the guide; my opinion is they add no value for the job. Local norms differ, so check yours |
| "References available on request" | Wastes a line; everyone knows |
| Buzzwords: "hard-working", "team player", "passionate" without proof | Show it with a bullet instead |
| Many weak bullets under one job | Keep the best 3 to 5 per recent job; fewer for old jobs |
| Fancy layout: columns, icons, progress bars | Risk for parsing; the guide says to keep the format commonplace |

## Before and after: five weak bullets

The jobs and numbers below are **made up by me** to show the pattern. They are not real results. Use your own true numbers; if you have none, use scope.

**1. A duty with no result**

- Before: `Responsible for maintaining the company website.`
- After: `Maintained a Next.js marketing site used by about 40,000 monthly visitors; fixed 25 accessibility issues found in an audit and cut the page weight of the home page from 4 MB to 1.5 MB.`
- What changed: an action, a scope and two results.

**2. A tool list**

- Before: `Worked with React, Redux, Node.js and MongoDB.`
- After: `Built a dashboard in React and Node.js that let support staff search 1.2 million tickets, replacing a spreadsheet process that took about 20 minutes per lookup.`
- What changed: the tools stay (good for keywords), but now they sit inside something that was built and mattered.

**3. A vague claim**

- Before: `Improved application performance.`
- After: `Reduced the p95 response time of the orders API from 900 ms to 350 ms by adding a database index and caching the product lookup in Redis.`
- What changed: what was measured, from what to what, and how. (The p95 is the value 95% of requests are faster than.)

**4. Teamwork with no detail**

- Before: `Helped the team with deployments.`
- After: `Set up a GitHub Actions pipeline that ran tests and deployed to staging on every merge, cutting release time from about 2 hours of manual steps to 15 minutes for a team of 6.`
- What changed: "helped" hides what you did. Name the thing you made.

**5. A big task with no outcome**

- Before: `Migrated database.`
- After: `Led the migration of a 200 GB MySQL database to PostgreSQL with no more than 10 minutes of planned downtime, and wrote the runbook the team used for two later migrations.`
- What changed: size, constraint, and a lasting result. Note it does not claim "zero downtime" unless that is true.

A mistake to avoid when you copy these: do not copy my numbers. If your honest line is "Fixed a flaky test suite so CI passed reliably", it is fine to leave it without a number than to invent one.

## Formatting summary

From the guide, collected in one place:

- Create it in **Microsoft Word or Google Docs**, then export a **PDF** with selectable text.
- No headers or footers in the file. Use **0.5 inch margins** to gain space.
- Fonts: **Arial, Calibri or Garamond**; at least **10 pt**.
- Standard section names, no symbols in headings.
- Dates as `MM/YYYY`; use `|` or tabs as dividers.
- One page.

Your file name is also read by humans. My opinion: `Firstname-Lastname-Resume.pdf` is clear.

## Cover letters, in one paragraph

The guide has a section on cover letters. Its advice is that a cover letter should **complement the resume, not repeat it**, be tailored to the company and role, be **under one page**, tell one short relevant story, and be proofread. Whether a given company reads cover letters varies; I did not verify how often they are read. If a job asks for one, follow that advice. If it is optional and you have no reason to write one, your time is probably better spent on the resume.

## Checklist

Print this and tick each line before you send a resume.

- [ ] One page; best points first.
- [ ] Headline of under 10 words and a summary of under 50 words that name the role.
- [ ] Name, city, personal email, phone and LinkedIn at the top; links open when logged out.
- [ ] Standard headings in a sensible order; no symbols in headings.
- [ ] Skills grouped (languages, frameworks, databases, tools); only things I can discuss.
- [ ] Jobs in reverse order with company, location, title and `MM/YYYY` dates.
- [ ] Every bullet starts with an action verb.
- [ ] Most bullets have a scope or a result; every number can be explained in one sentence.
- [ ] No duties-only bullets; no buzzwords without proof.
- [ ] Keywords from this job post appear where they are true, with abbreviations spelled out once.
- [ ] At least 2 projects with links, if work history is short.
- [ ] Created in Word or Google Docs; exported as a PDF; text is selectable.
- [ ] Plain-text paste test done: nothing missing, nothing garbled, order correct.
- [ ] Spelling and dates checked by someone else.
- [ ] If the company has a form, I filled it in carefully too.

## Self-check

Try to answer these without looking up. The short answers are below each question.

1. **How long should a software-engineer resume be, and why?**
   One page. The guide says a few best achievements beat many average ones, and short pages are scanned faster.
2. **What are the three parts of a strong bullet?**
   An action, a scope (how big) and a result (what changed, ideally with a number).
3. **What does the handbook guide say a bullet should look like?**
   "Accomplishment summary: action that resulted in quantifiable outcome."
4. **A bullet says "improved performance". How do you fix it?**
   Say what you measured, from what to what, and what you did. For example "cut p95 latency from 900 ms to 350 ms with an index and a cache".
5. **Name two format rules that help software read your file.**
   Export a PDF from Word or Google Docs with selectable text, and avoid headers/footers, symbols in headings and design tools. Standard fonts also help.
6. **Which ATS claims in the guide did I not verify?**
   That an ATS auto-rejects by rule, ranks by keyword count, and estimates years of skill from placement. They are the guide's claims.
7. **What is the plain-text test?**
   Paste your resume text into a plain text file and check that nothing is missing, garbled or out of order.
8. **Why should you only list numbers you can defend?**
   Interviewers ask about bullets. A number you cannot explain (how measured, compared to what) hurts trust more than having no number.

## Key takeaways

- Write for a short, distracted read; keep it to one page.
- Use the standard order and heading names, and a plain file that software can read.
- Every bullet: action, scope, result. Use true numbers you can explain.
- Take keywords from the job post only where they are true.
- Cut duties, buzzwords and old or unrelated items.
- Do the plain-text test before every send.
- Know the limits of the evidence: the guide is one experienced interviewer's advice, and the ATS claims are unverified.

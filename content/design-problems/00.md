> **In one line:** a design interview is graded on **how you think**, not on whether you reproduce a famous architecture. Run the same six steps every time: clarify, estimate, define the API, model the data, build the core algorithm, then stress it with scale and failure, and say your trade-offs out loud.

The other series in this track teach you the pieces. This one teaches you to **assemble** them under time pressure into a small, defensible design: a URL shortener, a rate limiter, pagination, an upload pipeline, notifications. These are *practical* design problems: more detail than a boxes-and-arrows whiteboard, closer to what you would actually build.

The big-picture version of this skill (load balancers, sharding, CAP) lives in the [System Design series](/system-design). Here we go one level lower: IDs, collisions, headers, indexes, failure modes.

## The six-step frame

```text
1. Clarify      What exactly are we building? Who uses it? What is out of scope?
2. Estimate     How big? Requests per second, data per day, read:write ratio.
3. API          What do clients call? Request, response, status codes, errors.
4. Data model   What do we store, and how is it accessed? Keys and indexes.
5. Core design  The central algorithm or component, with alternatives.
6. Scale & fail What breaks at 10x? What if a dependency is down? Trade-offs.
```

Keep a rough time budget for a 45-minute round: **5 minutes** clarify and estimate, **10** API and data model, **15** the core, **10** scaling and failure, **5** wrap-up and questions. Interviewers are often looking for you to *drive* the conversation through these steps.

## Step 1: Clarify requirements

Never start drawing. Ask questions, and write the answers where the interviewer can see them.

**Functional requirements** (what it does):

- What are the core actions? ("Create a short link; follow a short link.")
- Who are the users? Anonymous or logged in? Single user or many tenants?
- What is explicitly out of scope? (Say it: "I will not cover billing or an admin UI.")
- Any special features: custom aliases, expiry, analytics, bulk operations?

**Non-functional requirements** (how well it does it):

| Dimension | Questions to ask |
|---|---|
| **Scale** | How many users, requests per second, objects? Growth over time? |
| **Latency** | What is acceptable at p95/p99? (A redirect should be tens of ms; a report can take seconds) |
| **Availability** | What happens if it is down? 99.9% or 99.99%? |
| **Consistency** | Must everyone see the latest write immediately, or is eventual consistency fine? |
| **Durability** | Can we lose any data? (Payments: never. View counts: a few, maybe) |
| **Read/write ratio** | Read-heavy designs lean on caches and replicas; write-heavy ones on queues and sharding |
| **Security & abuse** | Who might misuse it? What must be private? |
| **Cost and team** | Is this a startup with two engineers or a large platform? It changes the right answer |

Restate the requirements in two lines before moving on: "So: authenticated users create links, anyone follows them, 100 million new links a month, 100:1 reads, redirects under 50 ms, 99.99% availability."

## Step 2: Estimate scale quickly

Back-of-the-envelope maths is not about precision; it is about **choosing the right order of magnitude** (one server, or a cluster?) and showing you can reason with numbers.

### Numbers worth memorising

| Fact | Approximation |
|---|---|
| Seconds in a day | **86,400 ≈ 10⁵** |
| Seconds in a month / year | ≈ 2.5 million / ≈ 30 million |
| 1 million requests per day | **≈ 12 requests per second** (1,000,000 / 86,400) |
| 100 million per day | ≈ 1,200 per second |
| Peak traffic | Average × **2 to 10** (use 3 to 5 if told nothing) |
| 1 KB, 1 MB, 1 GB, 1 TB | 10³, 10⁶, 10⁹, 10¹² bytes |
| 1 million rows × 1 KB | ≈ 1 GB |
| 1 billion rows × 1 KB | ≈ 1 TB |

Rough latencies: memory read about 100 ns; Redis round trip inside a data centre about 0.5 ms; SSD random read about 100 µs; database indexed query a few ms; cross-region round trip about 100 ms or more.

### A worked estimate

*"100 million new short links per month, reads are 100 times writes, keep links for 5 years."*

```text
Writes:   100M / month ÷ 2.5M s/month        ≈ 40 writes per second   (peak ~200)
Reads:    40 × 100                            ≈ 4,000 reads per second  (peak ~20,000)
Storage:  100M × 12 months × 5 years = 6 billion links
          × ~500 bytes per row               ≈ 3 TB
Cache:    80/20 rule: 20% of links get 80% of reads.
          Cache the hot 20% of one day's reads (~350M reads/day, say 100M unique links)
          20M entries × 500 B                ≈ 10 GB → fits in one Redis node's memory
Bandwidth: 20,000 reads/s × 500 B            ≈ 10 MB/s  (trivial)
```

Conclusions you can *say*: "Writes are small, reads are the problem, a cache removes most database load, and 3 TB fits comfortably on a few database nodes." That is exactly the type of sentence interviewers want.

## Step 3: Define the API

Describe the contract before the internals; it forces you to be concrete and it is where many design flaws appear.

- Resource names, methods, request and response bodies, **status codes**, **error format**, **auth**, **pagination**, **idempotency** for creates ([Backend, Lesson 02](/backend/lesson-2)).
- Note anything that affects design: "The redirect endpoint is public and extremely hot; the create endpoint is authenticated and rate-limited."

```http
POST /v1/links                      → 201 Created, Location: /v1/links/abc123
Content-Type: application/json
{ "url": "https://example.com/very/long?x=1", "alias": "launch", "expiresAt": "2027-01-01T00:00:00Z" }

GET  /abc123                        → 302 Found, Location: https://example.com/very/long?x=1
GET  /v1/links/abc123/stats         → 200 { "clicks": 1204, "byDay": [...] }
```

## Step 4: Model the data and its access patterns

Start from **how the data is read and written**, then choose the store and the keys.

1. **List entities and relationships** (User, Link, ClickEvent).
2. **List the queries**, with frequency ("lookup by code: 20,000/s", "list a user's links: 1/s").
3. **Choose the primary key and indexes** to serve the hottest queries.
4. **Choose the store**: relational (PostgreSQL) for relationships, transactions and flexible queries; key-value or wide-column (DynamoDB, Cassandra) for massive simple lookups; Redis for hot, small, ephemeral data; object storage for blobs; a search engine for text relevance. Say *why*. "PostgreSQL first; the access pattern is key-value, so we can shard or move to DynamoDB if volume demands."

```sql
CREATE TABLE links (
  code        text PRIMARY KEY,              -- the hot lookup key
  long_url    text NOT NULL,
  user_id     uuid NOT NULL,
  created_at  timestamptz NOT NULL DEFAULT now(),
  expires_at  timestamptz
);
CREATE INDEX links_user_created ON links (user_id, created_at DESC);  -- "my links"
```

## Step 5: The core design

Every problem has one or two **hard parts** (ID generation, the rate-limit algorithm, the pagination cursor). Spend your time there:

- State the problem the hard part solves.
- Present **two or three alternatives** with how each works.
- **Compare** them on correctness, complexity, cost and failure behaviour.
- **Pick one and justify it**, noting when you would pick a different one.

## Step 6: Scale, fail and trade off

Walk the system and ask "what breaks?" at each component:

- **One instance dies.** Is there a single point of failure? (Add redundancy and health checks.)
- **A dependency is slow or down.** Timeouts, retries with backoff, circuit breakers, fallbacks, **fail open or fail closed?**
- **Traffic is 10x.** Where is the bottleneck: database, cache, network? Scale reads with replicas and caches; scale writes by sharding or queueing.
- **Hot keys.** One popular item concentrates load on one shard or cache node.
- **Bad data and abuse.** Validation, rate limits, malicious inputs.
- **Duplicate or out-of-order messages.** Idempotency.
- **Observability.** Metrics (RED), logs, traces, alerts.
- **Consistency trade-offs.** Where is stale data acceptable?
- **Cost and operations.** How hard is it to run, deploy and debug?

### How to present trade-offs

A reliable sentence pattern:

> "Option A gives us X but costs Y. Option B gives us Z but costs W. Given [requirement], I'd choose A. If [requirement changes], I'd revisit and choose B."

## Common mistakes

1. **Jumping straight into boxes and technologies** without clarifying or estimating.
2. **Naming tools instead of reasoning** ("we'll use Kafka" with no stated reason).
3. **Ignoring non-functional requirements**, then designing something that cannot meet them.
4. **Over-engineering for Google scale** when the numbers say one PostgreSQL instance is plenty. State the numbers, then right-size.
5. **A single point of failure** that you never mention.
6. **No failure discussion**: "What if Redis is down?" has an answer.
7. **Silence**: not explaining your thinking. The interviewer cannot grade what they cannot hear.
8. **Treating the first design as final**: iterate, and welcome the interviewer's constraints.

## Interview questions

### Q1. What do you clarify first?

The core user actions and scope (and what is out of scope), then the non-functional requirements: expected scale, read/write ratio, latency targets, availability, consistency and durability needs, security and abuse concerns, and any special features. I restate them in a sentence or two so we agree before I design anything.

### Q2. How do you estimate scale quickly?

I convert business numbers to per-second rates (a million requests per day is about 12 per second), apply a peak factor, estimate storage as rows times bytes times retention, and size caches using an 80/20 access assumption. The goal is the order of magnitude, which tells me whether one machine is enough or whether I need caching, replicas or sharding.

### Q3. How do you present trade-offs?

For each important decision I name two or three options, state what each gains and costs against the requirements, choose one with a reason, and say what change in requirements would make me choose differently. That shows judgement rather than a memorised answer.

### Q4. What is the difference between a high-level and a practical design interview?

High-level system design covers broad architecture (load balancers, databases, sharding, queues) for a large product. A practical design interview goes deeper on one component: the ID scheme, the algorithm, the exact API, the schema and indexes, headers and failure behaviour, closer to what you would implement.

### Q5. How do you decide between SQL and NoSQL in a design?

From the access patterns and requirements: relationships, transactions and flexible queries point to a relational database; massive simple key lookups with predictable access patterns and horizontal scaling needs point to a key-value or wide-column store. I start with the simplest option that meets the numbers, and say how I would migrate if the numbers grow.

### Q6. What do you do when you do not know something in the middle of a design?

I say so, state the assumption I will use, and keep going: "I'm not sure how that provider rate-limits, so I'll assume X and design a retry with backoff that works either way." Clear assumptions and robust design beat bluffing.

## Key takeaways

- Use the **six steps**: clarify, estimate, API, data model, core design, scale and failure.
- **Numbers drive decisions**: convert to per-second rates, apply peak factors, size storage and caches.
- Start from **access patterns** to choose keys, indexes and stores.
- Spend your depth on the **hard part**: compare alternatives, choose, justify.
- Always discuss **failure modes and trade-offs**, and think out loud.

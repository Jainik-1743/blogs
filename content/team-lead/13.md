> **In one line:** career growth is mostly about **scope and impact**, not about how much code you write. A good lead knows what the next level means in their company, helps people take on bigger problems, speaks up for them in rooms they are not in (sponsorship), and keeps evidence so a promotion is a fair decision and not a surprise.

"How do you grow your people?" "What does a senior engineer do?" "Do you want to be a manager or a staff engineer?" These questions come up in interviews and in 1:1s. This lesson covers levels and ladders, what changes as people get more senior, the manager and individual contributor tracks, the staff-engineer path, how mentorship differs from sponsorship, and how to prepare a promotion case.

Read [Lesson 0](/team-lead/lesson-0) first for the answer shape. This lesson builds on [Lesson 8](/team-lead/lesson-8) (1:1s, feedback and delegation) and [Lesson 9](/team-lead/lesson-9) (mentoring, onboarding and hiring). For how leads think about the next role see [Lesson 4](/team-lead/lesson-4).

Everything here is **common practice and opinion**. Ladders and titles differ a lot between companies, so use the ideas, not the exact words, and always use your own true examples.

## Words used in this lesson

- **A career ladder** (or **leveling guide**) is a document that says what is expected at each level, so people and managers can talk about growth in the same terms.
- **A level** is one step on the ladder, for example "Developer 4". **Senior** usually means a level where the person works with little supervision and helps others.
- **IC** means **individual contributor**: someone who does the work and does not manage people. **The IC track** is the path for people who grow without becoming managers.
- **Scope** is how big and how unclear the problem is that someone owns. **Impact** is the result for the business, users or the team.
- **Output** is the amount of work done (tickets, lines of code). Output is not the same as impact.
- **A staff engineer** is, in many companies, an IC level above senior. The name and duties differ per company.
- **Mentorship** is advice and teaching from someone with more experience. **Sponsorship** is when someone with influence uses it to create chances for you, and speaks for you when you are not there.
- **A promotion packet** (or case) is a written summary of the evidence that someone already works at the next level.
- **A force multiplier** is a person whose work makes many others more effective.

## Levels and ladders: what they are for

A ladder is a shared language. Without one, "you are not ready to be senior" means whatever the manager feels that day. With one, the conversation can be about specific behaviours.

jorgef's open-source Engineering Ladders is a good example to read because it is short and public. What it says (I read it directly):

- It gives **four ladders**: Developer, Tech Lead, Technical Program Manager and Engineering Manager, over levels 1 to 7. Levels 4 and above are marked "senior".
- It describes growth along **five axes**: **Technology**, **System**, **People**, **Process** and **Influence**. Each axis has five steps, and "every level includes the previous one(s)".
- The **Influence** axis grows like this: *Subsystem*, *Team*, *Multiple Teams*, *Company*, *Community*. In the Developer ladder, D1 and D2 have *Subsystem*, D3 and D4 have *Team*, D5 has *Multiple Teams*, D6 has *Company* and D7 has *Community*.
- The **People** axis steps from *Learns* and *Supports*, to *Mentors*, then *Coordinates* and *Manages*.
- Its own FAQ says the framework "should not be used as a checklist to promote people but instead as guidance to have meaningful career conversations".
- It says companies usually expect a person to be **performing at the next level consistently for several months** before a promotion is made official.
- For evidence it suggests a mix of **1:1 conversations, feedback from peers and other teams, and self-evaluation**.
- It stops at level 7 because "levels 8 and above vary drastically from company to company".
- It says that examples of behaviour per level should be defined by each team, because they depend on the team's system and stack.

Notice what is missing from the five axes: lines of code and number of tickets. Growth is about what you own, who you help and how far your work reaches.

## Scope and impact beat output

The common thread in the sources I read is that moving up means a **bigger scope**, not more effort at the same size.

A simple way to picture it (my own words, loosely in the spirit of the impact-based levels in the list):

| Level of work | What it looks like | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Does a clear task | Someone tells you what to do and how | Fix this bug |
| Owns a project | You break down the work and finish it | Build the export feature |
| Owns an unclear problem | You decide what the problem is, then solve it | "Customers complain reports are slow" |
| Helps the team work better | Others get faster because of you | A test setup everyone uses |
| Helps several teams | Your work crosses team lines | A shared deployment path |

Two cautions:

- **More hours is not more scope.** A person who works late on small tasks is not working at the next level.
- **Impact needs a witness.** If nobody can say what changed because of your work, the case is weak. Numbers help: "release time down from 40 minutes to 12", "onboarding of new hires took 5 days instead of 10".

The Career growth section of charlax's list has an article on rewarding simplicity: "Nobody Gets Promoted for Simplicity" (I have only the one-line note: it has ideas for rewarding simple designs in promotions and celebrations). This is a good thing for a lead to think about. The person who prevents an incident, or who deletes code, often leaves little visible evidence. A lead can write that work down on their behalf.

## Two tracks: manager and IC

A healthy ladder lets people grow without becoming managers. In the list, Square's framework is described with two tracks and the note that **becoming a manager is not a promotion**. It is a different job.

jorgef's "Tech Lead vs Engineering Manager" page gives one clear split, which I read directly: the Tech Lead is in charge of the **system** and the Engineering Manager is in charge of the **people**. Some examples from its table:

| Tech Lead (system) | Engineering Manager (people) |
|---|---|
| Architecture and system integration | Career planning, promotions and coaching |
| Code reviews and technical feedback | One on ones |
| System SLAs, metrics and monitoring | Headcount planning and hiring |
| Hands-on coding 30% to 70% of the time | Hands-on coding 0% to 30% of the time |

The page also says that in a small team, one person may do both roles, and that each should be able to act in the other's role when needed. Treat the percentages as one framework's opinion, not a rule.

How to choose a track (my advice):

- **Try before you decide.** Lead a project, run the team's planning for a quarter, or mentor a new hire. See what gives you energy.
- **Management is a change of craft.** The work becomes meetings, 1:1s, hiring and hard conversations. If you love deep technical work, ask yourself honestly whether you will miss it. The list links Rob Walling's "Why Good Developers are Promoted into Unhappiness" (title and short summary only for me: it argues that management may not be for you).
- **You can move back.** The list links the idea of an "engineer/manager pendulum" (from an article on technical decay, title only). Many people go back and forth. It is not a failure.

When someone on your team asks "should I become a manager?", do not answer with a promotion. Help them try small pieces of the job and talk about it.

## The staff-engineer path

In many companies, "staff engineer" is the next IC step after senior. What it means differs per company, but the lists describe a few shared ideas (again, one-line notes only):

- charlax's professional-programming list has a **Getting to Staff Eng** section. One article in it says that "coding is a small part" of the job and that you should "figure out what matters and let the rest go".
- It links Will Larson's staffeng.com guides ("Being visible") and a post on **staff archetypes**. I could not open those, so I do not describe the archetypes.
- It also links a post by an engineer on how to find problems to solve at staff level.
- A post called "What's a senior engineer's job?" is described there as: you need to be more than just an individual contributor.

From jorgef's Developer ladder (which I read), the top levels add **multiple teams**, **the whole company** and **the community** as the reach of influence. The ladder also says levels above 7 differ too much between companies to describe.

My own working description of the staff-level shift, which is opinion:

1. **You pick the problem, not only the solution.** Nobody hands you a ticket. You find what matters most and write it down.
2. **You work through other people.** Your output is often a design, a decision or a better habit that others use.
3. **You write more.** Design docs, strategy pages and decision records (see [Lesson 5](/team-lead/lesson-5) and [Lesson 12](/team-lead/lesson-12)).
4. **You build trust across teams.** Not by title, but by being useful and honest.
5. **You still know the code.** Staff engineers who stop understanding the system lose their credibility.

As a lead, your job is to give such people real problems to own, protect their time from too many small tasks, and not use them as "the person who fixes everything".

## Mentorship is not sponsorship

Mentorship and sponsorship are different:

| | Mentorship | Sponsorship |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | Advice, teaching, a safe place to ask | Using your influence for someone |
| Where it happens | In private, with the person | In rooms the person is not in |
| Example | "Here is how I would prepare that design doc" | "Ask her to lead the migration. She did the hardest part last quarter" |
| Cost to you | Time | A bit of your credibility |

I did not find a source in the lists for this exact split, so treat the table as my own summary of a common idea. The related item in the lists is Keith Rabois's quote about expanding "the scope of their responsibilities until it breaks". The list also says that if people often visit a certain person's desk for help, that person may be ready for more responsibility.

As a lead you can sponsor in small, concrete ways:

- **Give visible work.** Let a person present at the demo, lead the incident review or own the cross-team project.
- **Say their name.** When a manager asks "who did this?", name the person and the result.
- **Open the room.** Invite them to a planning meeting where they will learn how decisions are made.
- **Recommend them.** When a stretch project comes, suggest the person, with your reason.
- **Be fair.** Sponsor based on evidence and potential across the whole team, not only those who are loud or similar to you. Watch for the biases in [Lesson 7](/team-lead/lesson-7).

## How to grow people on your team

A plain routine (my own, built on [Lesson 8](/team-lead/lesson-8) and [Lesson 9](/team-lead/lesson-9)):

1. **Ask what they want.** In a 1:1: "What do you want in 12 months?" Some want depth, some want to lead, some want stability. All are valid.
2. **Check the ladder together.** Look at the next level. Where are they already strong? Where is the gap? Use the ladder as a map for the conversation, not a checklist (as jorgef's FAQ says).
3. **Pick one stretch.** One project that is slightly too big, with support. Not three.
4. **Give feedback soon and specific.** After the stretch, say what worked and what to change.
5. **Make the work visible.** Presentations, docs, credit in public.
6. **Review every quarter.** Write down progress. Change the plan.
7. **Do not promise a promotion.** The list I read has a hiring entry that says a promise of an unconditional promotion is risky, because company conditions and performance can change. Promise a clear path and honest feedback instead.

> [!note] **Growth is not only upward.** Some people grow by becoming deeper experts, by switching area, or by spending a year as the team's mentor. A good lead notices these and values them. The list's "Titles are Toxic" entry (title only) is a reminder that titles are not the goal.

## Promotion packets and evidence

A promotion decision is stronger when it rests on written evidence gathered over time. jorgef's ladder says the usual rule is that the person has been performing at the next level **consistently for several months**. In other words, you promote a person for what they already do.

A promotion packet template (my own; your company will have its own format):

```md
# Promotion case: <name>, from <level> to <level>

## Summary (3 sentences)
What they do now at the next level, and why it matters.

## Scope and impact (the main section)
- Project 1: problem, what they decided, result with a number, who else was affected
- Project 2: ...
- Project 3: ...

## Behaviours against the ladder
For each ladder axis or competency: one or two real examples with dates.

## Feedback from others
Quotes from peers, other teams, product. Short and specific.

## Gaps and how they are being addressed
Be honest. A case with no gaps is not believable.
```

Tips:

- **Collect evidence all year.** Keep a running note per person: wins, feedback, incidents handled. The list suggests mixing 1:1 conversations, peer feedback and self-evaluation. Ask the person to keep their own record too (sometimes called a "brag document").
- **Use facts, not adjectives.** "Reduced the build from 20 to 8 minutes, now used by four teams" beats "great engineer".
- **Show scope growth.** "Owned a task" in January, "owned a problem" in June.
- **Check for bias.** Are you comparing the person to the same standard as others? Would you write the same words for someone you like less?
- **Share the result early.** The person should hear the plan before the committee, and hear honest reasons if the answer is not yet.
- **Know the process.** Who decides, when, and in what format. Ask your manager and HR early.

> [!warn] **Promotion, pay and level rules differ by country and company.** Talk to your manager and HR before you promise or say anything about outcomes. This lesson is about thinking, not about legal or pay advice.

When the answer is "not yet", give a precise gap and a plan: "To show the next level, I need to see you lead a project that crosses two teams. Let's choose one for next quarter." Vague answers hurt trust.

## Growing yourself as a lead

You also have a career. The lists have a few short ideas:

- **Take responsibility for your trajectory.** One article in the professional-programming list is summarised as: read books and papers, not only blog posts, and take responsibility for your career path (one-line note).
- **Ask for the scope you want.** This one is my own advice: read the level above yours, and ask your manager for a project that shows that scope.
- **Interview the company too.** awesome-cto's Career growth section links "reverse interview" resources and a GitHub list of reverse-interview questions (titles only for me).
- **Write your own ladder check.** Once a year, read your ladder's next level and mark each line as "done", "partly" or "not yet".

## Try it

1. **Output or impact?** A person closed 60 tickets this quarter. Another closed 12 but removed a flaky test setup that was costing the team 3 hours a week. Who has the stronger promotion evidence?
   **Answer:** It depends on the ladder, but the second person has concrete impact on the team, and the number is clear. Tickets alone show output. I would ask what the 60 tickets achieved.

2. **Mentor or sponsor?** You tell a junior how to structure a design doc. In a planning meeting you suggest she lead the next design. Which is which?
   **Answer:** The first is mentorship (private advice). The second is sponsorship (using your influence in a room she is not in, or is in without the standing yet).

3. **Not yet.** A senior engineer asks about the next level. Write a two-sentence answer for "not yet".
   **Answer:** "You do the senior work well. To show the next level, I need to see you pick and own a problem that crosses two teams, and bring others with you. Let's choose one for next quarter and I will help set it up." It names the gap and the next step.

4. **Evidence.** List three pieces of evidence you could gather this month for someone you want to promote.
   **Answer:** For example, a short note on a project they led with a measurable result, two pieces of peer feedback from outside the team, and a design doc they wrote that others used.

## Interview questions

### Q1. How do you help your team members grow? (Must know)

I start by asking what each person wants, because depth, leading and stability are all valid. Then we look at our ladder together, as a map and not a checklist, and find one strength and one gap. I give one stretch project at a time with support, give specific feedback soon after, and make the work visible with presentations and credit. I review progress each quarter and write it down. I never promise a promotion, but I promise honest feedback and a clear path.

### Q2. What is the difference between a senior engineer and a mid-level engineer? (Must know)

I say it depends on the company's ladder, and then I give my view: a mid-level engineer finishes well-defined work well. A senior engineer takes unclear problems, decides what the problem is, designs the solution, and makes others around them better through reviews, mentoring and docs. The change is bigger scope and wider influence, not just more code. In the public ladder I read (jorgef's), the influence axis moves from a subsystem, to the team, to multiple teams, and the People axis moves toward mentoring.

### Q3. How do you decide if someone is ready for promotion? (Must know)

I look for sustained evidence that they already work at the next level. In the ladder I read, the guidance is that the person performs at the next level consistently for several months. I compare their scope and impact to the ladder, collect feedback from peers and other teams, and use numbers where I can. I check my own bias by asking if I would write the same case for someone else. I do not promote for effort or for time served.

### Q4. What is the difference between mentorship and sponsorship? (Senior signal)

Mentorship is advice and teaching in private. Sponsorship is using my influence on someone's behalf, in rooms they are not in: recommending them for a project, naming them for results, giving them visible work. Mentorship helps a person grow; sponsorship helps their growth be seen and rewarded. I try to do both, and I try to be fair about whom I sponsor, based on evidence.

### Q5. A great engineer wants to become a manager. What do you say? (Senior signal)

I say it is a change of craft, not a reward. I ask what attracts them. Then I help them try small pieces: lead a project, mentor a new hire, run a planning session. I tell them honestly what management work looks like (1:1s, hiring, hard conversations) and that many people go back to IC work later and it is not a failure. If the company has a strong IC track, I explain that growth is possible without managing.

### Q6. What does a staff engineer do? (Senior signal)

It differs per company, and I say that first. In general, I see a shift to bigger scope: picking the problem and not just the solution, working through other people, writing design docs and strategy, and building trust across teams, while still understanding the code. I would not describe a specific company's staff role unless I had read it. I have read lists and guides that describe this shift, but I would check my own company's ladder.

### Q7. How do you handle someone who expects a promotion that you do not think is deserved? (Senior signal)

Early, honestly and with specifics. I show the ladder, say where they are strong, and name the gap with examples. Then we agree on a plan for the next quarter, with a project that would show the missing behaviour. I check back, and I write down what we agreed. I do not give a vague "keep working hard". If I think the process was unfair, I raise it with my manager and HR.

### Q8. How do you make sure promotions are fair? (Trap)

The trap is saying "we promote the best". I say fairness takes work. I use the written ladder, gather evidence from several people, and compare each case to the same standard. I look at who gets visible projects, because quiet people and people on less visible work can be missed. I check that work such as mentoring, simplification and incident prevention is counted, not only launches. I ask a peer lead to review my packet. And I accept that no process removes bias entirely.

### Q9. How do you write a good promotion case? (Senior signal)

I lead with scope and impact and use numbers: the problem, what they decided, the result, who else was affected. I map their behaviour to the ladder with dated examples, include short quotes from peers and other teams, and state gaps honestly, because a case with no gaps is not believable. I collect evidence throughout the year, so I do not rely on my memory in review season. I follow my company's format and ask HR about the process.

### Q10. Where do you want to be in three years? (Senior signal)

I answer with honest direction, not a title. For example: "I want to own a larger part of the technical direction and help more people grow. I am still deciding between staying close to the code and taking on more people management, so I am trying pieces of both." Then I give one thing I am doing now toward it. I also say what I want from my manager, which is clear feedback against our ladder.

## Key takeaways

- A ladder is a shared language for growth. Use it as a map for conversations, not a checklist.
- Moving up means bigger scope and wider influence, not just more output.
- Management is a different job, not a promotion. Good companies have a real IC track.
- The staff level, as the lists describe it, is about choosing problems, working through others and writing, while still knowing the system.
- Mentorship is private advice. Sponsorship is using your influence for someone in rooms they are not in. Do both and be fair.
- Promote people for what they already do consistently, and gather evidence all year.
- When the answer is "not yet", name the gap and agree on a concrete next step.
- Ladders and titles differ by company, and I read only one ladder fully. Check your own company's rules and ask HR.

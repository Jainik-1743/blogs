> **In one line:** a lead spends much of the week **explaining, aligning and planning** with people who are not developers. The skills are simple but not easy: start with the point, explain **why** before **what**, keep meetings few and useful, write things down, prepare people before you ask for a change, and plan around outcomes, not only output.

"Product wants it Friday." "Management asks why this is taking so long." "Can we skip the meeting?" "How do we change how the team works?" This lesson gives plain ways to handle these. It covers talking to stakeholders (the people who care about your work or are affected by it), running meetings, introducing change, and planning.

Read [Lesson 0](/team-lead/lesson-0) first for the answer shape, [Lesson 3](/team-lead/lesson-3) for estimates, and [Lesson 5](/team-lead/lesson-5) for writing design docs.

Everything here is **common practice and opinion**. Teams and companies differ a lot. Use the ideas, not the exact words, and always use your own true examples.

> [!note] **What I checked.** I read directly the Communication, Meetings, Change management, Planning, Product management and Trust sections of [charlax/engineering-management](https://github.com/charlax/engineering-management) (about 8.3k stars in an August 2026 snapshot) and the planning and estimates entries of the same list. **I only have the list's description of each article or book** (for example Paul Graham's "How to Disagree", the Amazon document culture article, *Simple Sabotage*, Basecamp's *Shape Up*, Melissa Perri's *Escaping the Build Trap*); I did not open them, and some entries are title-only. The templates and sample wording are mine.

## Words used in this lesson

- **A stakeholder** is a person or group who cares about the result or is affected by it: product, design, support, sales, management, users.
- **Alignment** means people agree on the goal, the plan and who does what.
- **Output** is what you build and ship. **Outcome** is the change it makes for users or the business.
- **A milestone** is a checkpoint on the way to a bigger goal.
- **An agenda** is the list of topics for a meeting, with the decision or result you need from each.
- **Nemawashi** is a Japanese word for quietly building support for a change with the people affected, before you propose it formally.
- **Process bankruptcy** means deciding to drop the old way of working and start with a small new one, instead of fixing every old rule.

## Talk about the why before the what

The communication section of the list has four useful ideas:

- **Narratives move people more than facts.** One article says to explain the **why before the what**, and to repeat it. In practice: "Support gets 40 tickets a week about this (why). So we will rebuild the page in two steps (what)."
- **Avoid the "apple pie" statement.** Shreyas Doshi's "Apple Pie Position" is described as an unobjectionable statement that makes the speaker sound good but adds little ("we should focus on quality"). Say something someone could disagree with, and give a reason.
- **Disagree about the point, not the tone.** Paul Graham's "How to Disagree", as the list describes it, aims to refute the central point rather than attacking tone or the person.
- **When trust is low**, an article advises: acknowledge the difficulty, speak tentatively, and aim for at least five positive interactions for each negative one.

### A short update that managers can use (my own)

```md
Status: on track | at risk | blocked
Done: <one line, in user terms>
Next: <one line>
Risk: <what could go wrong, how likely, what we are doing about it>
Need from you: <a decision or a thing, with a date>
```

Put the status and the ask at the top. A busy reader should be able to stop after two lines. Use numbers, not "a lot" or "soon".

## Meetings: fewer, shorter, written

Ideas from the list:

- **Cancel the meeting if you can live with the outcome.** An article with this title says: if you would accept the result without the meeting, skip it.
- **Write it down.** "The Document Culture of Amazon" is described as saying written documents reduce bias and avoid audio and screen-sharing problems. A one-page document read quietly can replace a long presentation. (See [Lesson 5](/team-lead/lesson-5).)
- **Know the anti-patterns.** *Simple Sabotage* (listed in the meetings section) covers meeting anti-patterns and how to fix them. I have not read it, so I do not list its points.
- **Better meetings.** Lara Hogan's "On Better Meetings" is listed with tips for efficient meetings but no specifics on the page.

A simple rule set (my own):

1. **Every meeting has a purpose and an owner**: "decide X" or "share Y". If it is "share", try a written update first.
2. **Send the agenda and the document before**, and say what decision is needed.
3. **Invite only those who decide or are affected.** Others read the notes.
4. **End with actions**: who does what by when. Send them the same day.
5. **Keep it short**, and stop early when the work is done.
6. **Automate the small things** so the meeting is not spent on bikeshedding (see [Lesson 7](/team-lead/lesson-7)).

## Introducing change: prepare people first

- **Nemawashi.** The change-management section of the list mentions it: quietly build support before you propose a change. Talk to the most affected people one by one, hear their worries, and change the proposal. When the formal discussion happens, there should be no big surprise.
- **Why the status quo is hard to change.** One article in the list discusses learned helplessness in teams ("nothing ever changes here") and suggests **declaring process bankruptcy**: stop trying to fix every old rule, and try a small, new, clear way of working.
- **Start small.** Try the change for one team or one month, measure, and then decide. See the reversible-decision idea in [Lesson 0](/team-lead/lesson-0).
- **Let people who disagree speak.** Resistance often carries real information about risks you missed.

A simple plan for a change (my own): *why now* (the problem, in numbers), *what changes* (small and concrete), *who is affected* (and talked to), *how we will know it works* (a measure), *how we go back* (if it fails), and *when we review* (a date).

## Planning: outcomes, milestones and honest dates

- **Objectives and milestones.** The list credits *High Output Management* (Andy Grove) with a simple frame: an objective is "where do I want to go", and milestones are "how will I pace myself".
- **Estimate, target, commitment, plan.** One article in the list separates these four words. Many fights come from one person saying "target" and another hearing "commitment". See [Lesson 3](/team-lead/lesson-3).
- **Estimating is still worth doing.** Two articles in the list ("Software Estimation Is Hard. Do It Anyway" and "Yes, You Should Estimate Software Projects") argue that estimates are necessary for planning, even though they are imperfect.
- **Fixed time, flexible scope.** *Shape Up* from Basecamp is listed as using fixed-length cycles and assigning whole projects to teams. Whether you like it or not, the useful idea is: when time is fixed and the work is bigger than expected, cut scope, not quality.
- **Outcomes over output.** *Escaping the Build Trap* (Melissa Perri) is cited in the list for its warning against valuing shipping over customer outcomes. Ask: "What should be different for users after this ships, and how will we see it?"

> [!note] **Ask the "so what" question.** Before you start a feature, ask the product owner: "If we ship this, what number or behaviour should change?" If nobody knows, you have found a risk before writing any code.

## Interview questions

### Q1. Product asks for a feature by Friday, and it will not fit. What do you do? (Must know)

I do not just say yes or no. I show what fits: a smaller first version by Friday, and the rest next week, or a later date for the full version. I explain the risk in their words ("if we skip testing the payment step, customers could be charged twice"). I ask what matters most: the date, the scope or the quality. Then they choose with the facts, and I write the choice down. If they still want all of it, I list the risks and ask for the decision in writing, but I do not trade away real safety or legal needs.

### Q2. How do you explain a technical problem to non-technical people? (Must know)

I start with the effect on users or the business, not the technology: "Checkout will be slow for about 5% of users on busy days." Then why it matters, then what we propose, the cost and the options, and what I need from them. I explain the **why before the what**, use an example or a simple picture, avoid jargon, and check understanding by asking them to tell me back in their words. I put the decision I need at the top.

### Q3. How do you give a status update when the news is bad? (Senior signal)

Early, plainly and with a plan. I say the status first ("at risk"), then the facts (what is done, what grew and why), the new range, the options (cut scope, move the date, add help), and what I recommend. I do not hide it in a long text. Telling someone early gives them room to choose; telling them late gives them only a problem.

### Q4. How do you decide whether a meeting is needed? (Must know)

I ask what the purpose is: decide, share or brainstorm. If it is only sharing, I try a short written update first. I follow the rule "cancel the meeting if you can live with the outcome without it". If I keep it, I invite only the people who decide or are affected, send the agenda and the document first, state the decision needed, and end with actions that have names and dates, sent the same day.

### Q5. How do you introduce a new process or tool to the team? (Senior signal)

I start with the problem in numbers ("reviews wait 2 days on average"). Then I talk to the most affected people one by one (nemawashi), hear their worries, and adjust. I propose a **small trial**, for one team or one month, with a measure and a way back. After the trial we review the numbers and decide. If the old way has too many rules to fix, I consider a small new start (process bankruptcy) instead of patching everything. I involve people who disagree, because they often see risks I missed.

### Q6. How do you handle a stakeholder who keeps changing requirements? (Senior signal)

First I find out why: new information, a changing market, or unclear goals at the start. I make the goal and "done" explicit in writing, and I keep a visible list of changes and their cost in time. I offer a regular moment to change priorities (for example at the start of each cycle) and a simple rule: if something comes in, something goes out, or the date moves. I stay calm and factual, and I tell them what the change really costs.

### Q7. How do you make sure the team builds the right thing, not only builds things? (Senior signal)

I ask for the outcome: what should change for users or the business, and how will we know? I try to get a simple measure before we start, and plan a check after the release. I like small releases that test the idea early. I raise it politely when we are building something nobody can explain the purpose of. The risk of valuing shipping over outcomes is a known warning in the reading lists I used.

## Key takeaways

- Start updates with the status and the ask. Use numbers.
- Explain the **why before the what**, and say something somebody could disagree with.
- Meetings: a purpose, an owner, the agenda and document first, only the people needed, actions with names and dates. Cancel if you can live without the outcome.
- Prepare people before a change (nemawashi), start small with a measure and a way back, and listen to those who disagree.
- Plan with objectives and milestones. Keep "estimate, target, commitment, plan" separate. When time is fixed, cut scope, not quality.
- Ask for outcomes, not only output: "What should be different after this ships?"
- This is common practice and opinion. Adapt it to your team.

> **In one line:** a **design doc** (or RFC) is a short written plan that you share **before** you build. It makes your thinking visible, lets others find holes while they are cheap to fix, and leaves a record of **why**. Big projects such as Rust, Python and Kubernetes all work this way.

In Lesson 1 and 2 you saw small decision notes. This lesson is about the bigger written plan: what goes in it, how real projects structure it, how to get feedback without endless arguments, and when **not** to write one.

Start with [Lesson 0](/team-lead/lesson-0) for the six-step answer shape. A design doc is that same shape, written down.

Everything here is **common practice and opinion**. Teams differ. Use the ideas, not the exact words, and always use your own true examples.

## Words used in this lesson

- **A design doc** is a short document that explains a plan: the problem, the proposal, the other options and the risks. You write it before the work.
- **An RFC (Request for Comments)** is a design doc that is shared for comments from a wider group, with a decision at the end.
- **A KEP** is Kubernetes' name for an enhancement proposal. **A PEP** is Python's name. They are RFCs with their own rules.
- **Goals and non-goals.** Goals are what the plan must achieve. Non-goals are things you will **not** do, written down so the discussion stays focused.
- **Alternatives** are the other options you considered, and why you did not choose them.
- **Prior art** is how other teams or projects solved the same problem.
- **Rollout and rollback** are how you release the change in steps, and how you go back if it goes wrong.

## Why write it down

The long reading lists that many engineers use agree on a few simple reasons (I summarise what the lists say about each article):

- **Writing is cheaper than building.** One article in the list says a few days of design writing can save weeks of wasted implementation, because writing exposes a flawed design early.
- **A written memo forces clearer thinking than slides.** Another says slides hide how ideas connect to each other; sentences do not.
- **A document culture helps everyone join in.** An article about Amazon's document culture says written documents reduce bias and avoid audio and screen-sharing problems in meetings.
- **People review at their own time.** A reader in another time zone can think before they answer.

> [!note] The Python PEP rules give another reason, in their own words about what a good PEP has: it records **why** the choice was made and **which ideas were rejected**, so the same arguments do not repeat every year.

## What real projects put in the document

Three public templates, side by side. They are different sizes, but you can see the same bones.

| Idea | Rust RFC | Kubernetes KEP | Python PEP |
|---|---|---|---|
| **What and why** | Summary, Motivation | Summary, Motivation | Abstract, Motivation (a weak motivation can get a PEP rejected) |
| **What is in and out of scope** | (in Motivation and Unresolved questions) | **Goals**, **Non-Goals** | Specification |
| **The proposal** | Guide-level explanation (teach it as if it already exists), Reference-level explanation (the technical design) | Proposal, Design Details | Specification, Rationale |
| **Risks** | Drawbacks | **Risks and Mitigations**, Drawbacks | (in Rationale) |
| **Other options** | Rationale and alternatives, Prior art | Alternatives | Rationale, **Rejected Ideas** |
| **How to prove it works** | (in the design) | **Test Plan**, **Graduation Criteria**, **Production Readiness Review** (observable, scalable, supportable, can it be rolled back?) | Reference implementation |
| **What is still open** | Unresolved questions, Future possibilities | (in sections above) | Open issues |

Two things stand out. Every one of them has a place for **why not** (drawbacks, alternatives, rejected ideas). And the biggest project (Kubernetes) adds a **production readiness** check: can we see it, scale it, support it and roll it back?

### A small-team design doc (my own mix)

For one team, one page is often enough. Use these headings and keep each part short:

```md
# Title: the decision, not the problem

Status: draft | in review | accepted | rejected | withdrawn | replaced by <link>
Owner: <name>     Reviewers: <names>     Decide by: <date>

## Summary            (3 sentences)
## Problem            (who is hurt, how much, how do we know)
## Goals / Non-goals  (what must be true / what we will NOT do)
## Proposal           (how it works, with one small example)
## Alternatives       (at least two, and why not)
## Risks              (what could go wrong, how we reduce each)
## Rollout / rollback (steps, flag or switch, how to go back)
## Test and monitoring plan (how will we know it works)
## Open questions
```

The status words come from Python's PEP process: **Draft**, **Accepted**, **Rejected**, **Withdrawn** (the author dropped it or chose another), **Deferred** (stalled, can come back), **Final** (done), and **Superseded** (replaced by a newer one). Using clear status words stops people asking "is this still true?".

## A simple process that works

1. **Talk first.** Have a short chat with one or two people before you write. The Rust process says the same: discuss the idea informally before you write the RFC.
2. **Write a short draft** and name one **owner** and the **reviewers**.
3. **Set a date.** Give people a fixed time to comment (Rust's "final comment period" is ten calendar days, see [Lesson 2](/team-lead/lesson-2)).
4. **Answer every comment**, even with "noted, not now". Change the document, not only the chat.
5. **Decide.** Python's PEP rules say one body has the final say (the Steering Council, who can delegate a single PEP to a person). In a team, say who decides before you start. You do not need everyone to agree, but write down the reasons.
6. **Record the outcome** and the rejected ideas. Link it from the pull requests.

> [!warn] **Do not write a design doc for everything.** The decision-record advice says to skip it when the decision is tiny, limited in scope, risk or cost, made by one developer, already covered by a standard, or temporary (a workaround or proof of concept). A design doc is for decisions that are big or hard to undo.

## Writing tips from the lists

The reading list has a small writing section. In simple words:

- **Cut words.** George Orwell's six rules (cut unnecessary words, prefer the active voice, avoid jargon) apply to design docs too.
- **Be specific.** One article says swap general words for specific ones: "slow" becomes "p95 of 2.3 s".
- **Short sentences, active voice.** This is the advice of Google's "Technical Writing One" course, as the list describes it.
- **Start with the answer.** Put the decision and what you need from the reader at the top.
- **Show an example.** The Rust template asks you to teach the idea "as if it were already part of the language". A tiny example makes a plan real.

## Interview questions

### Q1. When do you write a design doc? (Must know)

When the change is big, hard to undo, or touches other teams: a new service, a database change, a public API, a new library for the whole app. I skip it for small, local, easy-to-change work, where a good pull request description is enough. A simple test: "If I am wrong, how much will it cost to fix?" If the answer is "a lot", I write it down first.

### Q2. What goes into a good design doc? (Must know)

A clear problem (who is hurt and how we know), goals and **non-goals**, the proposal with a small example, **at least two alternatives and why not**, the risks and how to reduce them, a rollout and rollback plan, a way to measure success, and open questions. Real projects (Rust, Kubernetes, Python) all include a place for "why not", because the reasons for rejecting an idea are as useful as the idea itself.

### Q3. How do you get feedback without endless debate? (Senior signal)

I name an owner and a decider before I start, share the draft with a **date** for comments, and I answer every comment in the document. I split comments into "must fix before we start" and "can be decided later". If a thread goes round in circles, I take it to a short call and bring the outcome back into the document. When the date comes I decide, write down the reasons, and move on. People accept a decision they disagree with more easily when they can see their point was heard and recorded.

### Q4. What is the difference between a design doc, an RFC and a decision record? (Must know)

A **design doc** is the plan before the work. An **RFC** is a design doc that goes through a shared review process with a decision at the end. A **decision record (ADR)** is a very short note that records one decision, its context and its consequences, often written after (or from) the design discussion. The design doc says *how we think*; the ADR keeps *what we decided and why* for later readers. In a small team, one document can do both jobs.

### Q5. Someone raises a big objection after the decision. What do you do? (Trap)

First I listen: is this **new information** or a point we already weighed? If it is new and important, we reopen the decision, and I say so openly and update the document. If it is a point already in the "rejected ideas" section, I show where we discussed it and thank them. The Rust process works the same way: if major new arguments appear during the last comment period, the process stops and the proposal goes back to development; if not, the decision stands. I do not treat "the doc is approved" as a wall, and I do not reopen every decision for every complaint.

### Q6. How do you write a good status update or message? (Must know)

I start with the answer or the ask: "We are on track. I need a decision on X by Friday." Then the two or three facts that support it, then the risk, then the next step. I use short sentences, active voice, and numbers instead of "a lot" or "soon". I read it once as if I knew nothing about the project. If I cannot tell what the reader should do, I rewrite the top.

## Key takeaways

- Write the plan before the work when the change is big or hard to undo. Skip it for small, local, reversible work.
- A good doc has: problem, goals and non-goals, proposal, alternatives, risks, rollout and rollback, how you will measure success, open questions.
- Always include "why not" (drawbacks, alternatives, rejected ideas). It stops the same debate from returning.
- Use clear status words: draft, accepted, rejected, withdrawn, replaced.
- Name an owner and a decider, set a date for comments, answer every comment, then decide.
- Write short, specific, active sentences, with the answer first.
- This is common practice and opinion. Adapt it to your team.

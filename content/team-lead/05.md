A series about thinking should show where its ideas come from. This lesson is the reading list. It is also a good answer to the interview question "How do you keep learning?", because it names real sources instead of "I read blogs".

## How I picked the sources

I looked for two kinds of sources:

1. **Popular GitHub repositories** on engineering management, technical decisions, code review, reliability and open-source health. Stars are a rough sign that many people found a repository useful. They are **not** a sign of quality, and star counts change every day.
2. **Well-known documents** from teams that have run large engineering organizations in public: Google, Etsy, DORA, OpenSSF, the Rust project.

The numbers below come from web search results (third-party pages that copy GitHub data) and each snapshot has a different date. Treat them as "about this size", and check the repository page for today's number. Where I found no number, I say so.

## How to use a reading list (an interview-friendly way)

1. **Pick three, not thirty.** One about decisions (ADRs), one about review (Google's guide), one about incidents (blameless postmortems).
2. **Try one idea at work.** Write one decision record. Label your next ten review comments as Nit, Optional or FYI. Run one postmortem with "how" questions.
3. **Keep a short note** on what changed. That is your interview story: "I read X, I tried Y, the result was Z."
4. **Argue with it.** No list is the truth. Several sources above disagree (for example about "innovation tokens"), and that is healthy.

## Interview questions

### Q1. What do you read or follow to improve as an engineer, and what did you change because of it? (Must know)

I name two or three real sources, one idea from each, and one change at work. For example: "I read Google's public code review guide. I started labelling my comments as Nit, Optional or FYI, and I tried to reply within one working day. Our review waiting time went down." I do not list ten blogs. A short answer with a real change is stronger than a long list.

### Q2. A popular GitHub repo says one thing, and your team does another. What do you do? (Senior signal)

I treat stars as a sign of interest, not proof. I ask what problem the repo's advice was made for (a huge company, a startup, a database team?) and whether our situation is similar. Then I try the idea in a small, reversible way and look at the result. If it helps, we keep it and write down why. If it does not, I note that too. The aim is to fit our team, not to copy.

### Q3. How do you check that a claim in an article is true? (Must know)

I look for the primary source (the official documentation, the specification, the original research), not only a summary. I check the date, because tools and advice change. I try it on a small example. If I only read a summary, I say so: "I know this from a summary, I have not read the original." That honesty is part of being trusted.

## Key takeaways

- Popular repositories and documents are a good place to start, but stars are a rough signal only.
- Prefer primary sources: official docs, specifications, and the teams' own published guides.
- Say what you read directly and what you only know from summaries.
- Pick a few ideas, try them in a small way, and keep notes on the result. That becomes your interview story.
- All sources disagree somewhere. Think for your own team.

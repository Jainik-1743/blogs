> **In one line:** as a team grows, the problems change from "who writes this code" to "**who owns it, who gets woken up, and who talks to whom**". Good leads keep teams small, draw ownership on a map, run on-call so that it is humane and useful, and design team boundaries on purpose, because (as Conway's Law says) the software will copy them anyway.

A team of four shares everything in their heads. At fifteen people nobody knows who owns the billing job that failed at 3 a.m. At forty, two teams change the same module and each blames the other for the outage. This lesson covers how to split teams, how to write down ownership, how on-call works, how to avoid heroes, and how to manage dependencies between teams.

## Words used in this lesson

- **Ownership** means a named team (and, inside it, a named person) answers for a service or area: its quality, its incidents and its future.
- **Service catalogue** is a list of every service or system with its owner, purpose, links to its docs and its dashboards.
- **On-call** means being the person who is reachable to respond when something breaks outside normal hours or between tasks.
- **Rota (rotation)** is the schedule of who is on call when.
- **Runbook** is a short document with the steps to handle a known problem.
- **Toil** is repetitive manual work that grows with the service and could be automated.
- **Bus factor** is how many people can leave before something has no one who understands it.
- **Hero culture** is when the same few people save every crisis and the team depends on that.
- **Inverse Conway manoeuvre** means designing the **teams** to match the **software** you want, instead of letting the existing team shape decide your architecture.

## Splitting teams, and why Conway matters

From [Lesson 7](/team-lead/lesson-7): Conway's Law says a system's design tends to copy the communication structure of the organisation that builds it. The list of reading I used makes the point from the other side. Its entry on *Conway's Law in Team Topologies* is described this way: the "Reverse or Inverse Conway Maneuver" implies that we should design our **teams** (not the software yet) to get the software we want. So team boundaries are an architecture decision.

The same list has many articles on team structure (most are **title only**, so I do not describe them): Martin Fowler's *Team organization* articles, *Team Topologies* (it describes Fowler's summary of the book as "great"), *Organizing software teams*, *Independence, autonomy, too many small teams*, *Small teams* from an employee handbook, *Infrastructure Platform Engineering Organizational structures and models* (called "an incredible article" about structuring an infrastructure team), and *Spotify's Failed #SquadGoals*. The last title is a good warning in itself: copying another company's named model does not copy its context.

Principles that most of these sources and my own experience agree on (opinion):

1. **Keep teams small enough to talk.** A rule of thumb you may know is "two pizzas" (from the laws list in [Lesson 7](/team-lead/lesson-7)); Dunbar's number shows that people can only keep a limited number of working relationships. Fewer than ten is a common target.
2. **Split along the work, not along job titles.** A team that can deliver a change from idea to production without waiting for another team moves faster than "frontend team", "backend team" and "database team".
3. **Give each team a clear mission and a clear boundary.** If you cannot say in one sentence what the team is for, the boundary is wrong.
4. **Reduce hand-offs.** Each hand-off is where requirements get lost and queues form.
5. **Split when the pain is real:** when one team is always blocked by itself, when two parts have different release speeds, or when ownership is unclear and bugs fall between chairs. Do not split because the headcount reached a number.
6. **Do it in steps and explain why.** Reorganising causes anxiety. Say what problem you are solving, how people will be involved, and what will not change.

> [!warn] **Do not copy a model, copy the reasoning.** A structure that worked for a company with a hundred product teams may be wrong for a company with five. Ask: what problem did it solve, and do we have it?

## Ownership you can see

If nobody knows who owns a thing, nobody maintains it. Write ownership down:

**A service catalogue** can be as simple as a table in the repository:

```md
| Service | Purpose | Owner team | On-call | Docs | Dashboard | Tier |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| checkout-api | Takes orders | Payments | payments-oncall | /docs/checkout | link | 1 (revenue) |
| email-worker | Sends receipts | Platform | platform-oncall | /docs/email | link | 2 |
| internal-admin | Back-office UI | Support tools | business hours | /docs/admin | link | 3 |
```

Rules that keep it alive:

1. **One owner per service,** not "everyone". A group can own it, but a person is the contact.
2. **Put the file next to the code** and check it in reviews. A stale catalogue is worse than none.
3. **Tier the services** so you know which ones justify a page at night and which can wait until morning.
4. **CODEOWNERS files** (see [Git, Lesson 5](/git-github/lesson-5)) can send reviews to the right team automatically.
5. **Check ownership when people leave.** Orphaned services are how incidents turn into long ones.

The reading list also links to articles on production ownership. One in the on-call section, *Production Oriented Development*, is described as arguing that **engineers should own and operate the code they write in production**. That idea is a common reason to make developers part of the on-call rota rather than hand the pager to a separate team. (It is one view and it has costs, discussed below.)

## On-call that works

On-call is how a team honours its promise to users. Done badly, it burns people out. The list I used has several useful pointers (descriptions are the list's):

- **Alert on symptoms rather than causes.** From the article *My Philosophy On Alerting*: a page should mean "users are hurt or about to be", not "a disk is at 80%".
- **Remove noisy alerts.** *The Paradox of Alerts* is described as arguing that deleting most paging alerts can make systems better. Every false page teaches people to ignore the next one.
- **Tie monitoring to business metrics** (from a PagerDuty article on business metrics and outage response).
- **Default to rolling back** rather than fixing forward during an incident (from *Testing in Production: the hard parts*).
- **Measure incidents with clear terms.** *Incident Metrics in SRE* is described as defining time to detect, time to mitigate, time to recover and time between failures.
- **Use an incident commander.** The incident response reading includes *Incident Response at Heroku*, described as covering the Incident Commander role, inspired by disaster response.
- **Learn from the on-call chapter of the Google SRE Workbook** (it is on the list; I could not open it, so I do not describe it).

A humane on-call setup (my own checklist; the numbers are illustrations):

| Part | A reasonable starting point |
|---|---|
| Rota length | Often a week at a time; short enough to recover, long enough to learn. Choose with the team. |
| Rota size | Enough people that nobody is on call too often. If you have three people, the rota is a risk; fix it (more people, shared rota with another team, or lower service promises). |
| Pages | Only for user-visible symptoms. Anything else is a ticket for the morning. |
| Handover | A short note at the start and end of each shift: what happened, what is open, what looks odd. |
| Runbooks | One per alert. If an alert has no runbook, writing it is the first action after the incident. |
| Compensation and time | Time off or pay for out-of-hours work, as your law and company allow. Never expect it for free. |
| Review | A monthly look at every page: was it useful? Could it be automated? Could it be deleted? |
| Escalation | A clear second person and a way to reach the manager. Nobody should handle a bad night alone. |

> [!note] **Own it, but do not be a hero.** "You build it, you run it" can improve quality because people feel the pain of their own bugs. It also means every developer must be able to run on-call, so teams need training, runbooks and a limit. If a team's rota cannot be fair, change the work, not the person.

## Avoiding hero culture and the bus factor

- **Notice the hero.** If the same person fixes every crisis, ask what knowledge sits only in their head. Thank them and then **spread the knowledge**.
- **Pair on incidents.** Put a second person with the on-call, especially a newer one. Rotate who leads.
- **Write it down.** Runbooks, architecture notes and decision records ([Lesson 5](/team-lead/lesson-5)).
- **Count the bus factor** for each service in the catalogue. If it is one, name a second person and give them a real task.
- **Don't reward heroics, reward prevention.** Praise the person who removed the cause of the 3 a.m. alert more than the person who woke up and fixed it again.

## Toil

Toil is the quiet cost of growth: manual restarts, hand-edited config, copying data by hand, answering the same question. Habits:

1. **Name it and count it.** Ask the team to note in a shared list every time they do a repetitive manual task.
2. **Reserve time for it.** A fixed share of each cycle for removing toil is easier to defend than a one-off project.
3. **Automate the top item,** then re-count.
4. **Do not automate what you do not understand** yet. Write the steps as a runbook first; the runbook is the automation's specification.

## Dependencies between teams

When teams are separate, the connections between them are the risk. Practical habits:

- **Treat other teams like customers with a contract:** a documented interface, a version, a way to ask for changes and a way to report problems.
- **Make requests visible:** a shared queue or board, not a private message.
- **Say what you need and by when,** with the reason.
- **Plan cross-team work early:** list dependencies at the start of a quarter, with a named contact on each side.
- **Don't solve a dependency by going around it** (copying the other team's data, editing their code without agreement). Solve it with an agreed interface, or with an agreed change to the boundary.

The reading list has an article on *Organizational boundary problems: too many cooks or not enough kitchens?*, described as "a lot of useful resources to design organizations". I did not open it. Its title makes a useful pair of questions for your own boundaries: is a task shared by too many hands, or does a team lack the resources to finish it alone?

## Scaling the lead role

As the organisation grows, the lead's job moves:

- **From deciding to setting context.** You cannot be in every decision; give teams the goals and limits so they decide well.
- **From knowing everything to knowing who knows.** Keep the catalogue and the contacts up to date.
- **From doing to enabling.** Remove blockers, fix the process, and grow future leads ([Lesson 13](/team-lead/lesson-13)).
- **From talking to everyone to a few reliable channels:** a weekly written update, a regular all-hands, and 1:1s with your direct reports ([Lesson 8](/team-lead/lesson-8)).

The list has two scaling articles in its "Scaling an organization" section, both **title only**: *Scaling Without Imploding* and Stripe's *Atlas: Guide to scaling engineering organizations*. They are good next reads; I cannot say what they conclude.

## Interview questions

### Q1. How do you decide when to split a team? (Must know)

I split when the pain is real: the team is blocked by its own size, parts have different release speeds, or ownership is unclear and problems fall between people. I split along the work, so each new team can ship a change from idea to production with few hand-offs, and I give each one a clear mission. I explain the problem, involve people in the design and say what stays the same. I do not split because of a headcount number or because another company did it.

### Q2. What is Conway's Law and how do you use it? (Senior signal)

It says a system's design tends to copy the communication structure of the organisation that builds it. So I treat team boundaries as an architecture choice. The inverse manoeuvre is to design the teams to match the software I want. If I want a clean boundary between two services, I make sure two teams own them with a clear interface, rather than one team owning half of each.

### Q3. How do you make ownership clear? (Must know)

A service catalogue in the repository with one owner per service, its purpose, docs, dashboard, on-call contact and a tier. I check it in reviews, use CODEOWNERS so changes go to the right team, and update it when people leave. A group may own a service, but one person is the contact. Orphaned services are how incidents become long.

### Q4. How would you set up on-call for a team? (Must know)

I start with what deserves a page: user-visible symptoms only, with a runbook for each. I choose a rota that is fair, with enough people, a handover note and a clear escalation path, and I make sure out-of-hours work is compensated as the law and company allow. Monthly I review every page and delete or automate the useless ones. If the rota cannot be fair with the current team, I change the work or the promises, not just ask for more heroics.

### Q5. Your pager is too noisy. What do you do? (Senior signal)

I treat noise as a bug. I list every page for a month, mark which were useful, and delete or downgrade the rest, because false pages teach people to ignore real ones. I switch alerts from causes ("disk 80%") to symptoms ("checkout errors above x% for y minutes"), add a runbook to each remaining alert, and automate the repeated fixes. I track the number of pages per shift as a team health measure.

### Q6. What is a hero culture and how do you fix it? (Senior signal)

It is when the same people save every crisis and the team depends on them. It feels good and it is fragile: those people burn out, leave, and take knowledge with them. I thank them and then spread what they know: pair on incidents, rotate the lead, write runbooks and decision notes, and name a second person for each service. I praise prevention, like removing the cause of an alert, at least as much as rescue.

### Q7. Two teams keep blocking each other. What do you do? (Senior signal)

First I find out whether the problem is the **interface** or the **boundary**. If the interface is unclear, I help them agree an explicit contract: what each side provides, how to request changes, and how to report problems, with a shared visible queue. If the boundary is wrong (one change always needs both teams), I propose moving work or ownership so one team can finish it alone. I involve both leads, write down the decision and check in after a month.

### Q8. Should developers be on call for the code they write? (Senior signal)

Often yes, because feeling the pain of your own bugs improves quality, and the person who knows the code resolves incidents faster. But it only works with fair rotas, good runbooks, training, pages only for real user impact, and time off after bad nights. If the team is too small for a fair rota, I would fix that first, for example by sharing a rota across teams or reducing what the service promises.

## Key takeaways

- Team boundaries are an architecture decision, because software tends to copy communication structure. Design teams to match the software you want.
- Keep teams small, split along the work, give each a clear mission and cut hand-offs. Copy the reasoning of other companies, not their model.
- Write ownership down in a service catalogue with one owner per service, a tier and an on-call contact.
- Page only for user-visible symptoms. Review pages monthly, delete the noisy ones, and give each remaining alert a runbook.
- Spread knowledge to avoid heroes and a bus factor of one. Reward prevention.
- Count and remove toil with reserved time.
- Treat other teams as customers with a contract: documented interfaces and visible requests.
- This is common practice and opinion. Adapt it to your team.

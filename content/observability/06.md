> **In one line:** an incident is not won by the cleverest debugger. It is won by a calm team with clear roles, one place to talk, small safe fixes first (like a rollback), honest updates, and a **blameless** write-up afterwards that turns one bad night into a better system.

The page has fired. Somebody is awake. What happens next decides if the incident lasts 10 minutes or 3 hours. In most bad incidents the code is not the main problem. The main problem is people: five engineers each debugging the same thing in different chat rooms, nobody telling the customers anything, a risky fix tried at random, and nobody sure who is in charge.

## Words used in this lesson

- **An incident** is an event that hurts, or may soon hurt, users or the business and needs a coordinated human response.
- **Severity (SEV)** is a label for how bad an incident is. A lower number usually means worse (SEV-1 is the most serious).
- **Mitigation** means making the pain stop or shrink (a rollback, turning off a feature), even before you know the full cause.
- **Resolution** means the real problem is fixed and the service is healthy again.
- **Incident Commander (IC)** is the one person who runs the incident: keeps everyone coordinated, decides what to try, and speaks for the team. The IC does not usually fix things personally.
- **Deputy** is the IC's helper and hot standby.
- **Scribe** is the person who writes down what happens and when.
- **Subject matter expert (SME)** is someone who knows a specific system well and works on the fix.
- **Communications liaison** (customer liaison, internal liaison) is the person who writes updates for customers or for the rest of the company.
- **On-call** means being the person who will answer the page during a set period.
- **Escalation** means calling someone else (a second person, a manager, a specialist) when you cannot solve it alone or fast enough.
- **A post-mortem** (also called a retrospective or incident review) is a document and a meeting after an incident: what happened, why, and what to change.
- **Blameless** means the review looks at systems and decisions, not at who to punish.
- **Contributing factor** is one of several conditions that together allowed the incident. Most incidents have many. "Root cause" suggests there is only one, so I prefer this term.
- **Action item** is a concrete follow-up task with an owner and a due date.
- **MTTD, MTTA, MTTM, MTTR** are mean time to detect, acknowledge, mitigate and recover/resolve. We see below why the word "mean" can fool you.

## What counts as an incident

Not every bug is an incident. An incident is when something is going wrong **now** (or is about to) and one person alone cannot handle it. A useful working definition: users are hurt, or the error budget is burning fast, or data is at risk, and several people need to coordinate.

The big benefit of deciding "yes, this is an incident" quickly is that it switches the team into a different mode: one channel, roles, regular updates. The cost of calling it too early is small (a few people join and leave). The cost of calling it too late is big. So the habit is: **when in doubt, declare it. You can always downgrade.**

## Severity levels

A severity level tells everyone how serious the situation is, and how much effort and risk are justified. The PagerDuty documentation uses SEV levels where a lower number is more urgent, and says anything above SEV-3 is a **major incident** that gets a more intensive response. Their example meanings, in my words:

| Level | Meaning (PagerDuty-style example) | Typical response |
|---|---|---|
| SEV-1 | Critical. Many customers hurt, or long impairment that breaks agreements, or a security hole exposing customer data. | Full major-incident response, page an incident commander, tell executives, public notice |
| SEV-2 | Major. Real impact on a part of the service, but not total. | Major-incident response, coordinated team |
| SEV-3 | Minor. Limited impact, a workaround exists. | Handled by the owning team in normal flow |

Your levels will differ, and that is fine. What matters is that they are written down, short, and based on **user impact**, not on how scary the technology sounds.

Two habits from that documentation that I like a lot:

1. **If you are unsure which of two levels it is, treat it as the higher one.** During an incident is not the time to argue about the level. You can discuss it in the post-mortem. Raising the level costs a little. Lowering it too early costs a lot.
2. **A higher severity allows riskier fixes.** For a SEV-1 you may do things (a quick rollback in the middle of the day, a restart of the main database proxy) that you would never do for a SEV-3.

## Roles: why not everybody just fixes it

Imagine ten people in one room, all shouting ideas, and two of them typing commands on the same server. That is how a small incident becomes a big one. The fix is to give people **roles**, so each person has a clear job. The PagerDuty roles page describes these, which I summarise:

| Role | Job in one line | Important rule |
|---|---|---|
| **Incident Commander (IC)** | Runs the incident. Collects status, picks actions, delegates, is the one voice on what is happening. | The IC is **not a resolver**. They delegate the repairs and keep the big picture. |
| **Deputy** | Supports the IC: watches timers, remembers things the IC forgot, runs the call. A "hot standby" if the IC has to step away. | The deputy is an active role, not just an observer. |
| **Scribe** | Writes the timeline as it happens: key actions, status reports, decisions. | The scribe's notes become the base of the post-mortem. |
| **Subject matter experts (SMEs)** | People who know the broken systems. They investigate and do the repair actions the IC agrees to. | Keep talk flowing through the main SME of each system, so there is not 10 people talking. |
| **Customer liaison** | Writes and posts messages for customers (for example the public status page), after the IC approves. | Only one voice to customers. |
| **Internal liaison** | Keeps the rest of the company (executives, support, sales) informed, with short regular summaries. | Roughly every 30 minutes in PagerDuty's description. |

The same page makes a point I want to repeat: **the structure is flexible.** On a small incident one person may be IC, scribe and liaison. The idea is not "ten roles for every incident". The idea is that each of the jobs gets done, and that for any job, **everyone knows who is doing it**. For a small team of four, a good minimum is: one IC, one person fixing, and one person who writes down what happens and talks to others.

### The Incident Commander is not the best engineer

This surprises people. The IC is often not the person who knows the system best. The IC's skill is staying calm, asking good questions ("what changed in the last hour?"), keeping the group focused, and making a decision when people disagree. Their power comes from the role, not from rank. A junior engineer who is IC can tell the CTO, "please take that to the side thread". Having people trained in this role before the bad night is the best preparation.

If you join an incident and nobody is IC, **ask who is, and if no one is, say "I'll take IC" and announce it**. Taking charge for a few minutes is far better than leaving a hole.

## The first hour

A good first hour has a rhythm. This is my own checklist, built from the "during an incident" guidance I read and from common practice:

**Minute 0 to 5: stabilise the team**

1. **Acknowledge the page.** The sender must know that a human is on it.
2. **Check it is real.** Open the dashboard from the alert. Is the SLO page true? (A quick look, not a long study.)
3. **Declare an incident** and give it a **severity**. Open one channel (a chat room, plus a call if needed). Everyone goes to **that one place**.
4. **Say who is IC.** If you are alone, you are IC for now. Say it out loud in the channel.

**Minute 5 to 20: find a safe action, not the final answer**

5. **Ask: what changed?** Deploys, config changes, feature flag flips, traffic spikes, a dependency failing. Most incidents follow a change.
6. **Pick the quickest safe mitigation.** Roll back the deploy. Turn off the feature flag. Shift traffic away from a bad zone. Add capacity. Restart a stuck process. A good rule that matches the PagerDuty guidance: for a bad deployment, roll it back; for a stuck application, do a rolling restart; for a flood of events, check automatic throttling.
7. **Delegate each action to a named person**, one at a time, and have them report back ("Sam: roll back checkout to v2.40, tell me when pods are healthy").
8. **Write it down** (the scribe). Time, what, who.

**Minute 20 and later: communicate and confirm**

9. **Update stakeholders regularly**, even when there is no news ("still investigating, next update in 15 minutes"). Silence makes people panic and interrupt.
10. **Confirm recovery with the SLI, not with a feeling.** The error ratio and latency must go back to normal and stay there. Do not close the incident the moment the chart dips. Wait a short, agreed time (10 minutes is common).
11. **Declare it resolved**, say what the follow-up is (a post-mortem will come), and thank people.

### Mitigate first, understand later

The single most important idea of incident response: **stopping the harm comes before finding the cause.** If rolling back the last deploy restores service, do it, even if you have no idea why the deploy was bad. You can study the bad build later, in calm daylight, with all the logs, because you kept the evidence. Debugging a live fire for an hour while users suffer is a common mistake.

The rollback must be a safe habit. That is why release engineering matters: a rollback that works in two minutes is the best incident tool there is.

> [!warn] **Do not make unplanned risky changes in a panic.** "Let's just try restarting everything" can turn a partial outage into a total one. Before any action, the IC asks: "what do we expect to happen, how will we know, and how do we undo it?" Small, reversible steps first. Change only one thing at a time, or you won't know what worked.

### Things that waste incident time

- **Many channels.** Two rooms means two versions of the truth.
- **Too many people talking.** Let the SMEs talk, and others listen. Anyone can join to watch, but only those with a job should speak up.
- **Debating the severity or the cause** while the system is down.
- **The "hero" who goes quiet** and works alone for 40 minutes. Ask for a status every 10 or 15 minutes.
- **No handoff.** An incident that runs longer than 2 to 4 hours needs people to rotate. A tired person is a slow, error-prone person. The IC role itself should hand over, with a clear spoken handoff, "I'm handing IC to Priya. Current state is...".

## Communication during the incident

Customers and colleagues need three things: **what we know, what we're doing, and when we'll tell you more.** A short template that works for a status page post:

```text
[Investigating] Some checkout requests are failing. We are looking into it.
Next update in 30 minutes.

[Identified] We found a change that caused failures and are rolling it back.
Next update in 20 minutes.

[Resolved] Checkout is working normally. Some payments made between 14:09 and 14:46 UTC
may have failed. No card was charged for a failed order. A full report will follow.
```

Rules for such messages (matching the PagerDuty customer guidance I read, in my words):

- Be honest and plain. Don't guess the cause in public.
- Avoid the word "outage" unless it really was a full outage. "Incident" or "degraded service" is more accurate for partial problems.
- Give a time for the next update, and keep it, even if the update is "no news".
- One person (the customer liaison) posts, after the IC approves.
- Mention real customer impact in terms customers understand (what they could not do, for how long).

Inside the company, a short summary every 30 minutes for stakeholders keeps managers from joining the call to ask questions.

## On-call that does not burn people out

On-call means someone is always responsible for answering the page. It can be healthy or it can destroy a team. Here are the habits that make the difference.

**Structure**

- **A primary and a secondary.** If the primary doesn't acknowledge in a few minutes (5 is common), the page goes to the secondary automatically. Nobody should be alone with no backup.
- **A clear rotation**, usually one week per person, published in advance. People can swap shifts and plan their lives.
- **Not too small.** One person on call every other week is burnout. Aim for a rotation large enough that each person is on call a few weeks a quarter. If your team is too small, borrow people from a nearby team.
- **A written handoff** at the end of every shift: open issues, flaky alerts, things to watch.
- **Rest after a bad night.** If someone was paged at 3 a.m. for two hours, they don't start work at 9. Make it a rule and not a favour.
- **Pay or time off** for on-call duty. It is real work and real disruption.

**Habits for the person on call**

- Carry the laptop and a charged phone. Know the routes to the runbooks and dashboards **before** you need them.
- Test that your page actually reaches you (including "do not disturb" settings).
- **Escalate early.** Asking for help in the first 10 minutes costs little. Waiting 90 minutes to avoid "bothering" someone costs a lot. In PagerDuty's guidance for the call, the advice about the incident commander is "never hesitate to page the IC. It's much better to have them and not need them." A good team treats a page that wasn't needed as a normal, forgiven event.
- After every shift, **fix one thing**: improve a runbook, tune a noisy alert, write a small automation. On-call gets better only if the people doing it improve it.

**Health numbers for a rotation**

Track these every quarter: pages per shift, percent of pages that were actionable (we saw 3 of 48 in the last lesson, which is a team in trouble), pages outside working hours, and how long the average incident took. If the numbers are bad, fix the alerts, not the people.

## Time measures: what to track, and a trap

I wrote `incident-times.mjs` to compute the usual measures from a timeline. The timeline is the one from the fictional post-mortem later in this lesson. Output:

```text
time to detect  (start -> detected):    8 min
time to ack     (detected -> ack):      2 min
time to mitigate (start -> mitigated):  37 min
time to resolve (start -> resolved):    49 min
user impact window (start -> mitigated): 37 min
equivalent full-outage minutes: 14.1 of 43.2 => 32.5% of the 30-day budget
```

Read each line:

- **Time to detect** (8 min): from the first failing request to the page. Lower is better, and it is mostly an alerting question.
- **Time to acknowledge** (2 min): from page to a human taking it.
- **Time to mitigate** (37 min): from when users started suffering to when the pain stopped. **This is the number users feel.** It is the one to reduce.
- **Time to resolve** (49 min): from start to "all done and confirmed".
- **Equivalent full-outage minutes**: the impact (38% of requests failing for 37 minutes) is like 14.1 minutes of total outage, out of a monthly allowance of 43.2 minutes at 99.9%. So this one incident used about **a third** of the monthly error budget. That is the number to bring to the planning meeting.

> [!warn] **The trap of MTTR.** "Mean time to recover" averages all your incidents into one number. But incident durations are very uneven: many short ones and a few very long ones. An average of 20 minutes can hide one 6-hour disaster. Also the mean moves wildly with just a few incidents a quarter. If you must track a number, look at the **spread** (the median and the worst few) and the **user impact per incident**, or better, the budget used. Many SRE writers argue against using MTTR as a goal for exactly these reasons. I mention the idea as a warning, and I did not verify a specific source for it in this lesson, so treat it as an argument to evaluate, not a citation.

## Blameless post-mortems

After the incident, the team writes a document and holds a short meeting. The goal is learning. The PagerDuty process page says it in one sentence that I like: a post-mortem should be a "blame-free, detailed description of exactly what went wrong", with steps to stop it happening again, and it should also cover **how the incident response itself went**.

### What "blameless" means (and does not mean)

It does **not** mean "nobody did anything wrong" or "no accountability". It means we start from the belief that **people were doing their best with the information and tools they had at that moment**, and that a system that lets one person's single mistake cause a big outage is a system problem. If we punish the person, three bad things happen: people hide mistakes, people stop reporting near-misses, and the same system weakness stays in place for the next person.

Compare two sentences about the same event:

| Blaming | Blameless |
|---|---|
| "Dev A broke production by renaming a setting." | "A refactor renamed a setting. The old name silently fell back to a default of zero, and neither review nor tests caught it." |
| "The on-call was too slow to react." | "The page arrived at a time when the dashboard still looked normal, and the runbook did not say how to roll back." |
| "Ops should have noticed." | "We had no alert for retry volume, so the amplification was invisible." |

The blameless versions name **people by role** where needed, describe **conditions**, and lead directly to action items. You can still say who did what in the timeline (it is just facts) without any judgement.

A useful question to replace "who did this?" is **"what made this seem like a reasonable thing to do at the time?"** The answer is nearly always something about the system: a confusing name, a missing guard, a doc that was out of date.

### Contributing factors, not a single root cause

Almost no serious incident has one cause. There is the trigger (the thing that started it), and there are the conditions that let the trigger hurt (no limits, no tests, no alert, no runbook). Writing "root cause: human error" is useless, because it ends the thinking exactly where learning should begin. List several **contributing factors** and fix as many as are worth the cost. A popular tool is the "five whys": keep asking "why did that happen?" until you get to something you can change in the system. Use it as a thinking aid, and be careful not to force a single chain when the truth is a web.

### The process (adapted from the PagerDuty process page)

1. **The IC names an owner** for the post-mortem right after the incident. The owner is not necessarily the person who fixed it.
2. **Schedule the meeting first**, then write. PagerDuty's rule: within 3 calendar days for a SEV-1 and 5 business days for a SEV-2. Memory fades quickly, so don't wait weeks.
3. **Start with the timeline.** Use the scribe's notes. For each item, link to the **data**: a graph, a log search, a message. If you can't link evidence, mark the line as "from memory".
4. **Analyse.** Capture the impact (numbers!), what happened, and why.
5. **Share a draft** a day before the meeting, so people can comment in advance and the meeting is short.
6. **Hold a short meeting** (15 to 30 minutes in PagerDuty's description) to agree on the facts and the actions. It's not for debate about guilt.
7. **Create action items** as tickets with owners and dates. PagerDuty's advice is to avoid creating too many; keep to the ones that really must be done. A list of 30 actions guarantees none get done.
8. **Share the lessons** with the wider company and keep the document where people can find it.
9. **Follow up.** Check weeks later that the actions were done. A post-mortem whose actions are never completed teaches people that post-mortems are theatre.

The PagerDuty template sections are: owner, meeting time, call recording, overview, what happened, contributing factors, resolution, impact (with exact numbers), responders, timeline (with a data link per line), "how did we do" (what went well, what did not), action items, and the messages to send. It also has a status field that moves from draft, to in review, to reviewed, to closed. I use the same skeleton in the example below, with a few changes.

### What makes a good action item

Bad: "Be more careful with config." Good: "Make the HTTP client refuse unknown config keys at startup (owner: Dana, due 2026-03-28, ticket PLAT-512)."

Classify actions so that you cover several layers, not only prevention:

- **Prevent** it from happening again (a guard, a test, a rule).
- **Detect** it faster (an alert, a dashboard).
- **Mitigate** it faster (a rollback tool, a kill switch).
- **Process** (a runbook change, training).

And for each, say **how you will know it worked**.

### Post-mortem: Checkout failures after the v2.41 release

| | |
|---|---|
| **Status** | Reviewed |
| **Date of incident** | 2026-03-14 (Saturday) |
| **Severity** | SEV-2 |
| **Post-mortem owner** | Dana Okafor |
| **Incident commander** | Omar Haddad |
| **Scribe** | Priya Nair |
| **On-call responder** | Mia Chen |
| **Meeting** | 2026-03-18, 30 minutes |

#### Summary

On Saturday 14 March 2026, between 14:09 and 14:46 UTC, about 38% of checkout requests failed for 37 minutes. The cause was a combination of a brief slowdown in the payments service and a release of `checkout-api` (v2.41) in which the payments client was accidentally changed to retry **five times with no delay**. The retries multiplied the load on the payments service and turned a two-minute slowdown into a 37-minute incident. We rolled back to v2.40, and error rates fell within minutes. The incident used about 32% of the 30-day error budget for checkout.

#### Impact

| Measure | Value |
|---|---|
| User-visible impact | Checkout page showed "Payment could not be processed" |
| Duration of impact | 37 minutes (14:09 to 14:46 UTC) |
| Average checkout error ratio during impact | about 38% |
| Checkout requests during impact | about 88,000 |
| Failed checkout requests | about 33,000 (many users retried by hand, so failed *orders* were fewer: an estimated 9,100 shoppers did not complete a purchase on first try) |
| Money charged for failed orders | None. Payment calls that failed were never captured. We checked the ledger. |
| Error budget used | about 14.1 equivalent full-outage minutes of a 43.2 minute monthly budget (32.5%) |
| Data loss | None |

(All numbers in this example are invented for teaching. The error budget line comes from the real arithmetic in `incident-times.mjs`: 0.38 x 37 = 14.1 minutes.)

#### What happened

`checkout-api` calls `payments-svc` to authorise a card. Before the release, the client used up to 3 attempts, with exponential backoff and random jitter, as described in the platform guide.

Release v2.41 (deployed at 14:02 UTC) included a refactor that moved HTTP client settings into a shared config file. The key `backoffMs` was renamed `backoff_ms`. The code that reads the config **ignored unknown keys** and used a default of **0 ms** when the setting was missing. At the same time the default number of attempts was changed from 3 to 5 in the same refactor. The result: after any failed payment call the client retried immediately, up to five times.

At about 14:08 `payments-svc` had a short slowdown (about 2 minutes) while a database replica was promoted. Normally, the old client would have backed off and the slowdown would have passed with no customer impact. With v2.41, every slow or failed call became up to five calls in under a second. Our traffic to `payments-svc` rose to roughly four to five times normal. Because `payments-svc` was now overloaded, **its calls kept failing even after the database was healthy**, which caused more retries. The system stayed in a bad stable state until we removed the retries by rolling back.

#### Contributing factors

1. **Silent config default.** An unknown or missing key fell back to 0 ms without an error or a log line.
2. **No retry budget or backoff test.** Our tests checked "retries happen", but not "retries wait". No test covered the shape of retry traffic.
3. **Retries at one level, without a limit.** Nothing capped total retry volume, such as a circuit breaker, a retry budget or load shedding on `payments-svc`.
4. **Brief dependency slowdown.** The trigger. It was ordinary and expected, and the system should have absorbed it.
5. **Release timing.** The release went to 100% of pods at once, 7 minutes before the slowdown. A staged rollout (5%, then 25%, then 100%) would have limited the blast radius and made the connection to the release more obvious.
6. **Detection delay.** The page came 8 minutes after the first failures. Errors grew as the retry load built up, and the fast-burn alert needs both a one-hour and a five-minute window to be above the line and has a 2 minute `for` clause.
7. **Runbook gap.** The checkout runbook said "check dependencies" but not "check what was just deployed", and had no rollback command.

#### Resolution

At 14:24 the on-call responder declared a SEV-2 and Omar took the IC role. At 14:41 the team compared the shape of outgoing traffic before and after the deploy and noticed the five-fold jump in calls per checkout, and found the new retry setting. At 14:46 the rollback to v2.40 completed. Error rates fell within a few minutes. The incident was closed at 14:58 after the error ratio had been under the SLO line for 10 minutes.

| Time | Event | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| 14:02 | v2.41 of checkout-api is rolled out to 100% of pods | Deploy log #4471 |
| 14:08 | `payments-svc` slows down during a database replica promotion | payments-svc latency dashboard |

#### What went well

- The page arrived and was acknowledged within 2 minutes, and a clear IC took over within 7 minutes of the page.
- Roles were used. Priya's notes made this timeline easy to build.
- The customer liaison posted honest updates with the next update time.
- Rollback worked on the first try. Nobody tried to hot-fix forward.
- The ledger check confirmed no customer was charged for a failed order.

#### What did not go well

- It took 32 minutes from the first failure to find the cause. We looked at `payments-svc` first. The checkout team's own deploy was not at the top of the checklist.
- Nobody suspected "our retries are making it worse" until the traffic multiplier was seen on a graph. We had no panel for "outgoing calls per incoming request".
- The status page was updated 24 minutes after impact started.

#### Where we got lucky

- It happened on a Saturday morning with lower traffic than a weekday peak. At weekday volume the payments service would likely have crashed rather than just slowed down.
- A payment provider call that fails is not captured, so there was no double charging.

#### Lessons learned

1. A retry is a bet that the dependency will be healthier in a moment. Without a delay, a limit and a jitter, the bet becomes a weapon pointed at your own dependency.
2. Silent defaults are dangerous. A system that starts with a wrong setting and says nothing is worse than a system that refuses to start.
3. When something is slow, check what *we* changed and what *we* are sending, before blaming the dependency.

#### How we kept this blameless

We name people only by role in the narrative. The config rename was reviewed and approved; the review process did not show the effect of an ignored key, and nothing in the tooling could have shown it. The question we asked was "what made this look safe?" The answer was a quiet default and an incomplete test, so the actions fix those, not the person.

That is a full post-mortem. It's about two pages. Notice what it contains: **numbers**, **links to evidence**, **several contributing factors**, **luck stated openly**, **action items with owners, dates and a way to check them**, and **no person is blamed**.

> [!warn] **Do not copy my post-mortem's neatness into real life.** Real ones are messier: facts are uncertain, the timeline has gaps, and people disagree. A good post-mortem says "we do not know" where that is true, and creates an action to find out.

## Common mistakes with incidents and post-mortems

- **No owner.** Without a named owner, the post-mortem is never written. Assign it at the end of the incident.
- **A post-mortem only for "big" incidents.** Near-misses teach cheaply. Write short versions for them.
- **A blame hunt in disguise.** Phrases like "should have known" or "failed to" point at a person. Rewrite them as conditions.
- **Action items that are wishes.** "Improve monitoring" is not an action. "Add alert X by date Y, owner Z" is.
- **Too many actions.** Choose the few that really reduce risk and track them to the end.
- **Never reading old post-mortems.** Keep them searchable, and look through them when you design a new system or plan a game day.
- **Skipping the "what went well".** Leaving out the good things means you may later "fix" them away.
- **Forgetting to update runbooks and alerts.** The document is only useful if the system changes afterward.

## Try it

1. Write a one-paragraph definition of "incident" and a three-level severity table for a system you work on. Base each level on user impact.
2. Pretend a bad deploy breaks your app right now. Write the first ten minutes as a numbered list: who acknowledges, who is IC, which channel, what is the first mitigation, and how will you know it worked?
3. Rewrite these blaming sentences as blameless ones: (a) "Sam pushed a bad migration." (b) "The on-call engineer ignored the alert." (c) "QA missed this bug."
4. Take the timeline in the example post-mortem. If the alert had paged at 14:12 and the rollback was done at 14:30, recompute time to detect, time to mitigate and the equivalent full-outage minutes (error ratio 38%).
5. Pick one real post-mortem from a public list and find: the trigger, two contributing factors, one thing that went well, and one action you would add.

### Answers

1. Any answer that is short, written down, and based on user impact is good. Example: SEV-1 (most users cannot use the core feature), SEV-2 (a large group or an important feature is hurt), SEV-3 (small impact with a workaround).
2. A good answer: acknowledge the page, declare an incident and open one channel, name an IC (or yourself), check what changed (the deploy), roll back, confirm with the error ratio, post an update. It is the same shape as the first-hour list.
3. (a) "A migration shipped that locked a table, and our review and staging setup did not show the lock time." (b) "The alert fired during an unrelated noisy period and the on-call had learned to expect false pages, so it took longer to treat as real." (c) "Our tests did not cover this path, and we have no check that new features include tests for failure cases."
4. Time to detect = 14:12 minus 14:09 = **3 minutes**. Time to mitigate = 14:30 minus 14:09 = **21 minutes**. Equivalent full-outage minutes = 0.38 x 21 = **8.0 minutes**, which is 8.0 / 43.2 = about **18.5%** of the monthly budget (versus 32.5% in the real timeline). Faster detection and rollback saved about 14 percentage points of the budget.
5. Open-ended. A good check is that every contributing factor you list is about a *system* or a *condition*, not a person.

## Interview questions

### Q1. Walk me through what you do in the first ten minutes of a serious incident. (Must know)

I acknowledge the page so the system knows a human is on it. I take a quick look at the dashboard to see that the problem is real. I declare an incident with a severity, open one channel, and say who is the incident commander. If I'm alone that is me. Then I ask what changed recently, such as deploys, config, flags or traffic, because most incidents follow a change. I look for the quickest safe mitigation like a rollback, assign it to a named person, and write down what we do. After that I post a first status update with a time for the next one.

### Q2. What does an incident commander do, and what do they not do? (Must know)

The IC coordinates. They get everyone in one place, collect status, decide which actions to try, delegate each action to a person, and speak for the team. They also approve external messages. They usually do **not** do the repair themselves, because their attention has to stay on the whole picture. The role is about process and judgement, not about being the best engineer. A deputy helps with timers and notes and can take over if the IC needs to step away.

### Q3. What is the difference between mitigation and resolution? Which comes first? (Must know)

Mitigation stops or reduces the user pain, for example a rollback, a feature flag off, moving traffic away, or adding capacity. Resolution is the proper fix and confirmation that everything is healthy. Mitigation comes first. If rolling back the last deploy fixes the problem, I do it before I fully understand the bug, and I keep the logs and evidence to study afterwards. Users do not care about our understanding, they care about the pain stopping.

### Q4. What does "blameless" mean in a post-mortem? Does it mean no accountability? (Must know)

No. Blameless means we assume people acted reasonably with the information and tools they had, and we look for what in the system allowed a single mistake to cause harm. Accountability stays, but it is about doing the follow-up work, not about punishment. If we punish, people hide mistakes and near-misses, and the weakness stays. I like the question "what made this seem a reasonable thing to do at the time?" because the answer usually points to a fixable cause such as a confusing name or a missing guard.

### Q5. Why avoid the phrase "root cause"? (Senior signal)

Because most serious incidents have several contributing factors: a trigger, plus conditions that let it hurt, such as missing limits, missing tests, slow detection and a runbook gap. "Root cause: human error" ends the thinking just where learning should begin. I list several contributing factors and decide which are worth fixing. The five whys is a useful aid, but I do not force the truth into one chain.

### Q6. How would you write a good action item? (Senior signal)

It is specific, has an owner and a due date, and says how we will know it worked. "Improve monitoring" is a wish. "Add an alert for outgoing calls per incoming request, owner Mia, due 28 March, verified by a promtool test" is an action. I also make sure the set covers different layers: prevent, detect, mitigate and process. And I keep the list short, because 30 actions means none get done. I follow up later to check they were completed.

### Q7. How do you decide severity during an incident? (Must know)

By user impact and the risk to data or the business, using written definitions, not gut feeling. If I can't decide between two levels I treat it as the higher one and review it afterwards, because the cost of acting too strongly is smaller than the cost of acting too weakly. A higher severity also tells everyone that riskier fixes are acceptable, for instance a rollback in peak hours.

### Q8. Your team's on-call is burning people out. What do you change? (Senior signal)

I first measure: pages per shift, how many were actionable, how many were outside working hours, and how long the incidents ran. Usually a handful of noisy alerts cause most of the pain, so I delete or downgrade the non-actionable ones and automate mechanical fixes, as we discussed for alerting. Then I look at the structure: a primary and a secondary, a rotation large enough that nobody is on call too often, written handoffs, rest after night pages, and compensation. And I make sure every shift ends with at least one small improvement, so the load goes down over time.

### Q9. MTTR went down this quarter. Is that good news? (Trap)

Not necessarily. MTTR is a mean over a small and very uneven set of incidents. A single 6-hour incident or the absence of one can move it a lot, so it can improve or worsen by luck. It also hides the spread and ignores how much user impact each incident had. I would look at the median and the worst incidents, at time to mitigate for each, and at how much error budget the incidents used. I would also check whether we simply started counting different things as incidents.

### Q10. A senior engineer is the incident commander, and the CTO joins the call and starts giving orders. What do you do? (Trap)

I keep to the roles. The IC is the single authority on the incident and the CTO can advise or ask questions through the IC. If the CTO wants to take command, they should say so clearly and the IC hands over formally, so everybody knows who is in charge. Two people giving instructions is dangerous. I would also make sure the CTO gets regular updates through an internal liaison, which usually removes the urge to jump in.

### Q11. How do you communicate to customers during an incident? (Must know)

One person owns customer updates, after the IC approves them. I post early ("we are investigating"), state what customers can't do, and always give the time of the next update, even if the update is "no news". I avoid guessing at the cause in public, and I avoid the word "outage" for a partial problem. After the incident I send a short summary and apology, and a link to a fuller report if the incident was large. Honest and regular beats perfect and late.

### Q12. What goes into a good post-mortem timeline? (Senior signal)

Facts with times in one time zone (UTC), each with a link to the evidence such as a graph, a log search or a chat message. It includes when the contributing change happened, when impact started, when the page fired, when it was acknowledged, each significant action, when mitigation happened, and when the incident was closed. Where the time comes from memory I mark it as such. This makes it possible to measure detection and mitigation times and to see where time was lost.

### Q13. You find during the post-mortem that the real fix would take three months and a lot of money. What do you do? (Senior signal)

I write the fix down honestly, and then also write cheaper actions that reduce the risk now: better detection, a faster mitigation, a limit on the blast radius, a runbook. I make the trade-off explicit with the product owner using the error budget: how much budget does this risk cost per quarter, and what is the cost of the fix? Some risks are accepted on purpose, with an owner and a date to look again. What I won't do is leave it unwritten.

## Key takeaways

- An incident needs **roles** (IC, deputy, scribe, experts, liaison), **one channel**, and **regular updates**. The IC coordinates and does not usually fix.
- **Mitigate first, understand later.** A fast, safe rollback beats clever live debugging.
- When you are unsure about severity, treat it as the higher one.
- Make small, reversible changes, one at a time, and confirm recovery with the SLI.
- On-call should be sustainable: primary and secondary, real rotation size, handoffs, rest, and improving one thing every shift.
- Track detection, acknowledgement, mitigation and resolution times, and **budget used per incident**. Beware of the mean (MTTR).
- A blameless post-mortem asks what in the system made the mistake easy. It lists **contributing factors**, not one root cause, and has a short list of action items with owners and dates.
- Retries and timeouts appear in many real post-mortems, including our fictional one. The next lesson shows how to get them right.

> **In one line:** a team lead is not checking whether you know the "best" tool. They are checking whether you **think before you choose, say your reason in simple words, and know what could go wrong**. Every good answer has the same shape: *goal, options, choice, reason, risk, plan B*.

Picture a code review. You added a new package, and your lead writes: "Why this package?" Or you picked a different language for a small service, and the lead asks: "Why this language?" Or you went quiet for an hour, and the lead asks: "What are you thinking?"

These are not traps. They are the normal questions of a team that wants to stay healthy. A lead asks them because they will own the result with you, long after the code is merged. If you can answer them calmly, you sound like someone who can be trusted with bigger work. That is the 4+ years signal.

This lesson gives you the questions, a simple way to answer each one, and the mindset behind them. It also helps if **you** become the lead: these are the questions you will start asking others.

Everything here is **common practice and opinion**, not law. Teams differ. Use the ideas, not the exact words. For stories about your past work see [Experience round, Lesson 14](/fullstack-qa/lesson-14). For people questions (conflict, salary, offers) see [Behavioural, salary and offers, Lesson 38](/fullstack-qa/lesson-38). For tools like `npm audit` in a pipeline see [Deployment and CI/CD, Lesson 26](/fullstack-qa/lesson-26).

The ideas that come from named sources (Google's code review guide, Dan McKinley's "Choose Boring Technology", Michael Nygard's decision records, Google's SRE book, and DORA metrics) I checked through web search summaries. I could not open the original pages directly, so I **paraphrase** them and do not quote long text. Open the originals before you quote them in an interview.

## Words used in this lesson

- **A trade-off** is when you get one good thing by giving up another. Faster to build, but harder to change later. There is almost never a choice with no cost.
- **A dependency** is code written by someone else that your project needs, like an npm package. **A direct dependency** is one you added. **A transitive dependency** is one your dependency added for you.
- **Maintenance** is the work to keep something working after the first release: updates, fixes, security patches.
- **A risk** is something that might go wrong. Think of two numbers: how likely it is, and how bad it would be.
- **A reversible decision** is one you can undo cheaply (change a variable name, try a library on one page). **A hard-to-reverse decision** is one that is expensive to undo (database choice, public API shape, a language for the whole backend). Spend more thinking on the second kind.
- **An assumption** is something you believe is true but have not checked. Good thinkers say their assumptions out loud.
- **A spike** is a short, time-limited experiment to learn something ("one afternoon to try this library on a real page"). You throw the code away afterwards if needed.
- **A proof of concept (PoC)** is a small version that shows an idea can work. It is not production code.
- **A decision record (ADR, Architecture Decision Record)** is a short note that says what was decided, why, and what it costs. It helps people who join later.
- **A postmortem** is a written review after an incident. **Blameless** means it looks for causes in the system and the process, not for a person to punish.
- **Bus factor** is how many people can leave (or be sick) before the project is in trouble. A bus factor of one is a risk.
- **Technical debt** is a shortcut that saves time now and costs time later, like a loan with interest.

## What a team lead is really checking

When a lead asks "why", they usually want one of these five things. Know which one it is:

| What they ask | What they really want to know |
|---|---|
| "Why this package?" | Did you compare options, and will we regret it in a year? |
| "Why this language or framework?" | Can the team run and hire for it, or is it your hobby? |
| "What are you thinking?" | Is your plan clear, and can I help if you are stuck? |
| "How long will it take?" | Do you understand the work, and will you warn me early if it grows? |
| "What if it fails?" | Did you think about the bad day, not only the good day? |

> [!note] A "why" question is not an attack. It is an invitation to show your thinking. If you take it as a fight, you answer with defence ("it is popular!"). If you take it as a chance, you answer with a reason. The second version always sounds stronger.

## The answer shape: six short steps

You can answer almost any "why" question in about 30 seconds with this shape. Say it in plain words:

1. **Goal.** "We needed to ..." (the real need, not the tool).
2. **Options.** "I looked at A, B and writing it ourselves."
3. **Choice.** "I chose B."
4. **Reason.** "Because ..." (one to three facts, not feelings).
5. **Risk.** "The risk is ..., and I reduce it by ..."
6. **Plan B.** "If it does not work, we can ... and it costs about ..."

Example for a date library:

> "We needed to show times in the user's time zone. I looked at the built-in `Intl` API, `date-fns` and `dayjs`. The built-in `Intl.DateTimeFormat` was enough for display, so I added nothing new for that part. For the one place that adds months to a date, I used `date-fns` because it is small, has TypeScript types, and each function is imported on its own. The risk is another dependency to update. I lowered it by pinning the version through the lockfile. If we drop it later, it is used in one file, so the exchange is cheap."

Notice what is missing: no "everyone uses it", no "it is the best", no long speech. Just reasons a teammate can check.

> [!warn] **"Because it is popular" is not a reason by itself.** Popular is a fact about the crowd, not about your problem. Say what popularity gives you in this case: more answers online, more people who know it, faster bug fixes. Then it becomes a real reason.

## "Why this package?"

Adding a package is borrowing someone else's code and promising to keep up with it. Before you add one, ask these questions. A lead is asking the same ones in their head.

**1. Do I need it at all?**
Can the platform do it already (`fetch`, `URL`, `structuredClone`, `Intl`, `crypto.randomUUID`)? Can I write it in 20 clear lines and test it? Small helpers are often cheaper to own than a dependency. Big, hard problems (dates across time zones, parsing, cryptography, forms with many rules) are usually worth a library, because the edge cases are many and you do not want to find them one by one.

**2. Is it alive and cared for?**
Look at the last release date, open issues that nobody answers, how many people maintain it, and whether the project has a clear way to report security problems. A package with one tired maintainer and no release for three years is a risk even if it has many downloads. Check with commands you can run yourself:

```bash
npm view some-package time.modified version license   # last change, latest version, license
npm view some-package dependencies                    # what else it pulls in
npm audit                                             # known problems in what you installed
npm outdated                                          # what is behind
npm explain some-package                              # why is this installed? (who needs it)
```

**3. Is it a good fit for our stack?**
Does it have TypeScript types? Does it work with our framework version and our runtime (Node version, browser, edge)? Does it work with Server Components, or does it need the browser? A package that fights your stack costs more each week.

**4. What does it cost to carry?**
Bundle size for browser code, install time, how many transitive dependencies it brings, and the license (can we legally use it in our product?). Every extra dependency is one more place for a security problem or a breaking change.

**5. How hard is it to leave?**
If this package dies, how many files must change? A good habit: wrap it in one small module of your own, so the rest of the code calls *your* function and not the package everywhere. Then replacing it is a one-file job.

**6. Who on the team can support it?**
If only you understand it, the bus factor is one. Pick things that more than one person can read, or write a short note on how it is used.

> [!note] **A simple rule many teams use:** add a dependency only when it saves more time than it will cost over the life of the project, *including the day it breaks*. Say that number out loud, even if it is a rough guess.

A short decision note for a new package can be just this (a mini version of a decision record):

```md
# Use date-fns for date arithmetic

Status: accepted

Context: We add months and days in the billing screen. Native Date makes this
easy to get wrong near month ends.

Decision: We will use date-fns, imported per function, in `lib/dates.ts` only.

Consequences: Easier: correct month maths, typed API. Harder: one more
dependency to update. We can swap it by editing one file.
```

This follows the usual shape of a decision record (title, status, context, decision, consequences) that Michael Nygard described. The point is not the paperwork. The point is that the "why" is saved for the next person, who will otherwise ask the same question again.

## "Why this language or framework?"

This question is bigger and costs more to get wrong. Most of the time the honest best answer is **boring**.

**The "boring technology" idea.** Dan McKinley wrote that a team has only a small number of "innovation tokens" (he suggested about three). Every new, unproven technology spends one. Well-known tools such as PostgreSQL have known failure modes, lots of documentation, many people who know them, and tested tools around them. So spend your tokens only where a new tool gives you a real advantage for the product, and be boring everywhere else. (Some writers disagree with the "tokens" idea and say the real cost is how hard something is to *operate*, not just how new it is. That is a fair point: think about running it at 3 a.m., not just writing it.)

Questions to answer before you pick a language or framework:

1. **What does the problem need?** Heavy maths and data work, a quick web API, low-level speed, a small script? Start from the need.
2. **What does the team already know?** A language the team knows well is "boring" for *this* team, and that is a good thing. A better language that nobody knows can be slower overall.
3. **Can we hire and onboard for it?** In a year, can we find people who can work in it? Is learning material easy to find?
4. **Is the ecosystem ready?** Libraries, tools, hosting, monitoring, testing. Check that the things you need already exist and are maintained.
5. **How will we run it?** Build, deploy, logs, errors, security updates. A new language also means a new pipeline and new things to learn at 3 a.m.
6. **How hard is it to change later?** A language for one small, separate service is easy to undo. A language for the core of the product is not.
7. **Is it a learning goal or a business goal?** It is fine to want to learn something new. Say it, and try it on something small and safe, not on the payment system.

Example answer when you pick the same language as the rest of the stack:

> "I used TypeScript on the server so we share types and validation with the front end, and so everyone on the team can review it. A Python service might be nicer for the data job, but then we need a second build, a second deploy and another set of dependencies to patch. If the data work grows, we can split it out later behind the same API."

And an honest answer when you really need something different:

> "This job is a heavy image conversion that blocked our Node process. I moved it to a worker using a tool made for it, behind a queue. The cost is one more thing to deploy and watch, so I wrote a short runbook. I kept it as a separate, small service so the main app is not affected."

> [!warn] **Do not choose a tool to improve your CV.** Leads notice quickly. It is fine to be excited about a new tool, but the reason you say out loud must be about the **product and the team**.

## "What are you thinking?"

This question has two very different meanings:

- **Friendly:** "You look stuck. Can I help?"
- **Doubtful:** "This change looks odd. Explain it."

In both cases, the best response is to **think out loud** in a simple, tidy way. Silence makes people guess, and guesses are usually worse than the truth. Use this small pattern:

> "Right now I'm trying to **[goal]**. I **know** [fact 1] and [fact 2]. I **don't know** [unknown]. My **guess** is [assumption], and I'll check it by [next small step]. If that's wrong, I'll try [other idea]."

Example while debugging:

> "I'm trying to find why the page is slow for some users. I know the API answers in 80 ms, so it's not the server. I don't know yet whether it's the browser or the network. My guess is a big JSON response, and I'll check the response size in the network tab. If it's small, I'll look at rendering next."

Why this works: you show a *method*, not a mood. The lead can correct a wrong assumption in ten seconds ("the API is cached, check without cache") instead of watching you spend two hours.

> [!note] **Say when you are stuck, and say it early.** A common rule of thumb: try alone for a fixed short time (many teams say 30 to 60 minutes), write down what you tried, then ask. Asking with notes ("I tried A and B, I saw X") respects the other person's time. Staying silent for a day is the expensive mistake.

## Other questions a lead asks (and what they mean)

You will hear many versions of these. The *meaning* is almost always the same: "Did you think about the cost and the bad day?"

| Question | What a good answer includes |
|---|---|
| "What problem does this solve?" | The user or business need, in one sentence. If you cannot say it, stop and find out. |
| "What is the simplest thing that could work?" | Start small. Say what you would *not* build yet, and why. |
| "What else did you consider?" | At least two options and why you dropped them. |
| "What happens if we do nothing?" | The real cost of waiting. Sometimes the answer is "nothing much", and that is useful. |
| "What could go wrong?" | Two or three real risks (data loss, security, slow, hard to undo), and how you reduce each. |
| "How will we know it works?" | A test, a metric, or a log you will check. Not "it looks fine". |
| "How do we roll it back?" | A simple, tested way back (feature flag, previous deploy, reversible migration). |
| "Who else does this affect?" | Other teams, other services, support, users. Name them and say you told them. |
| "How long will it take?" | A range with the unknowns named. Not one exact day. |
| "What do you need from me?" | A clear ask: a decision, a review, access, or time. |
| "Is this a one-off or will it repeat?" | Whether to patch it or fix the cause. |
| "Why now?" | Why this is more important than the other things in the list. |
| "What did you learn?" | One real lesson, not "nothing, it went well". |

## Thinking about size and speed: estimates

A lead needs dates for planning, and you need to protect yourself. A few honest habits:

- **Give a range, and name the unknowns.** "Three to five days. The two days of risk are the payment provider's test account, which I have not used yet."
- **Break work into small pieces.** If a piece is longer than two or three days, it is still too vague to estimate well. Split it.
- **Include the invisible work:** tests, review, deploy, docs, and fixing what you find on the way.
- **Update early.** If the estimate changes, tell the lead the same day. Early bad news is cheap; late bad news is not.
- **Do a spike for big unknowns.** "I need a half day to learn whether this library can do X. Then I can give a real estimate."

For more on estimates as a story see [Lesson 38](/fullstack-qa/lesson-38).

## Reviewing code like a lead

If you review other people's code, you are already doing a lead's job. A few ideas from public engineering guides, simply put:

- **Aim for "better", not "perfect".** Google's public code review guide says a reviewer should lean towards approving a change once it clearly improves the overall health of the code, even if it is not perfect. There is no perfect code, only better code. Waiting for perfection slows the whole team.
- **Mark small, optional comments as such.** The same guide suggests starting optional remarks with "Nit:" so the author knows they do not have to fix it in this change.
- **Review the change, then the person's time.** Reply quickly. A slow review is a hidden cost for everyone.
- **Ask questions before you give orders.** "What made you choose this approach?" often teaches more than "change this", and sometimes the author has a good reason you missed.
- **Say what is good.** One honest, specific compliment is not flattery; it tells the author what to repeat.
- **Keep it about the code.** "This function is hard to follow" is better than "you write confusing code".

> [!note] A good review comment has three parts: **what** you see, **why** it matters, and **an option** (not only a complaint). "This query runs inside a loop, so it will run 100 times for 100 users. Could we fetch all rows at once with `IN (...)`?"

## When things go wrong: the leader's mindset

A lead is judged most on the bad day. Habits to copy:

- **Fix first, explain after.** During an incident, the first job is to stop the pain (roll back, switch the flag off). Find the deep cause once users are safe.
- **Tell people early and plainly.** "We have a problem with checkout. We are rolling back. Next update in 15 minutes." Silence makes things worse.
- **Be blameless, not careless.** Google's SRE book describes blameless postmortems: they focus on the causes in the system and the process, and assume people acted reasonably with what they knew. The reason is practical: if people are punished, they hide problems, and then the same incident returns. Blameless does **not** mean "nobody is accountable". It means you ask "how did our system let this happen?" and not "who do we blame?".
- **End with actions, not just words.** Each postmortem should have a few concrete follow-ups with an owner and a date: a test, an alert, a safer deploy step.

For a full incident story and examples see [Debugging, Lesson 10](/debugging/lesson-10).

## Measuring a team without hurting it

Leads are often asked "how is the team doing?" A well-known research group, DORA, describes four simple measures of delivery:

- **Deployment frequency:** how often you ship.
- **Lead time for changes:** how long from a finished change to it running in production.
- **Change failure rate:** how often a release causes a problem that needs a fix or rollback.
- **Time to recover:** how fast you fix a problem after a bad release. (DORA has used slightly different names for this one over the years, so check its current wording before you quote it.)

The first two are about **speed**, the last two about **stability**. The useful idea is that good teams improve *both*, not one at the cost of the other. Small, frequent releases are usually safer than big, rare ones.

> [!warn] **Do not turn measures into a scoreboard for people.** If you reward "most commits" or "most tickets", people will game the number and the real work suffers. Use measures to find *problems in the process*, then talk to the team.

## Growing into the lead role

When you start to lead (even without the title), the job changes from "write code" to "help the team make good decisions". Habits that help:

- **Give context, not only tasks.** Say *why* the work matters and what "done" looks like. People make better choices when they know the goal.
- **Delegate with a clear owner.** One name per task. Say how much freedom they have and when to check in. Do not take the work back at the first sign of a mistake.
- **Teach, don't rescue.** When a junior is stuck, ask "what have you tried?" first. Then give the smallest hint that helps.
- **Make decisions, and write them down.** Waiting for 100% certainty is itself a decision, and usually a slow one. For reversible choices, decide quickly. For hard-to-reverse ones, take more time, ask for opinions, and write a short decision record.
- **Say "no" with a reason and an option.** "We can't do this by Friday without skipping tests. We can ship the basic version on Friday and the rest next week. Which do you prefer?"
- **Protect the team's focus.** Fewer parallel tasks, fewer surprise changes, honest priorities.
- **Take responsibility up, share credit down.** When something fails, you say "we" and own it with your manager. When something works, you name the people who did it.
- **Keep learning in public.** Say "I was wrong about that" when it is true. It makes it safe for others to do the same.

## Interview questions

Each answer below is a model in the first person. Put your own true example inside the shape.

### Q1. Why did you choose this package? (Must know)

I start with the need: "We had to do X." Then I say what I compared: the built-in way, two or three packages, and writing it ourselves. I give two or three facts for my choice: it is maintained (recent releases), it fits our stack (TypeScript types, works with our framework version), it is small, the license is fine, and few extra dependencies come with it. Then I name the risk and the exit: "If it dies, it is used in one file, so we replace it there." I do not say "everyone uses it" as my only reason.

**Follow-up: "What if the maintainer stops working on it?"** I check how widely it is used and whether there is a fork. I keep the package behind my own small wrapper, pin the version, and I watch security advisories. If it stays important, we can replace it or take over the small part we need.

### Q2. When would you write something yourself instead of adding a package? (Must know)

When the need is small and clear, the platform can almost do it, and I can test it well. For example, a 15-line helper for grouping an array. I do **not** write my own for hard problems with many edge cases, such as dates and time zones, cryptography, parsing, or sanitising HTML. There, a well-tested library is safer than my version. The question I ask is: "Which one costs less over the life of the project, including the day it breaks?"

### Q3. How do you check that a package is safe to use? (Senior signal)

I look at several things, not one. Maintenance: last release date, how fast issues are answered, number of maintainers. Security: `npm audit`, known advisories, and whether the project has a way to report problems. Size: how many transitive dependencies it brings. License: can we use it in our product? Trust: is the name exactly right? (Fake packages with names close to real ones exist.) I install with the lockfile committed and use a clean install in CI so that versions do not change without review. I also avoid packages that run install scripts without a good reason.

### Q4. Why did you choose this language or framework? (Must know)

I follow the same shape: need, options, choice, reason, risk, plan B. Usually my reasons are the team's skills, hiring, the ecosystem, and how we run it in production. When there is no strong reason to be different, I choose the boring option, because then the team spends its energy on the product. If I choose something new, I say which "innovation" I am spending and why this problem deserves it, and I start with a small, separate piece that is easy to undo.

**Follow-up: "What if the team wants to move to a new language?"** I ask what problem it solves today. I suggest a small experiment (a spike) with clear success rules and a time limit. I count the whole cost: learning, tooling, hiring, a second pipeline, and two codebases for a while. If the benefit is clear, we move step by step, not in a big rewrite.

### Q5. The team disagrees with your technical choice. What do you do? (Must know)

I ask them to explain their view first and I listen for facts I missed. Then I put the options side by side with the trade-offs, in a short document or a table, so we argue about the options and not about people. If we can test it cheaply, we run a small spike. If it is reversible, we pick one and set a date to check how it went. If not, we involve whoever owns the decision. Once a decision is made, I support it even if it was not mine. That is part of being a good team member.

### Q6. What are you thinking right now? (Must know)

I say the goal, what I know, what I do not know, my best guess, and my next step. I do this out loud, in short sentences. This turns a silent, worrying moment into a short chat where the lead can help me.

### Q7. You are stuck on a problem. When do you ask for help? (Must know)

After a fixed, short time of honest effort (for example, 30 to 60 minutes, depending on how urgent it is). Before asking I write down what I want, what I tried, and what I saw (error text, steps to reproduce). Then I ask a specific question. I also say what I will try next while I wait. If the work is blocking the team or a release, I ask sooner.

### Q8. How do you estimate a task? (Must know)

I break it into small pieces. For each piece I think about the known work and the unknowns. I give a range and name the biggest risk. I include tests, review, deploy and fixing. For big unknowns, I ask for a short spike first. I update my estimate as soon as I learn something that changes it, and I tell the lead the same day.

### Q9. You find out you will miss a deadline. What do you do? (Must know)

I tell the lead as early as I can, with facts: what is done, what is left, why it grew, and a new range. I bring options: cut scope, move the date, add help, or ship in two steps. I do not hide it and hope. Early honesty gives the team room to choose; late honesty only gives them a problem.

### Q10. How do you decide between a quick fix and a proper fix? (Senior signal)

I ask how bad the damage is now, how often it happens, and how risky the proper fix is. If users are hurt now, I do the quick, safe fix first (a rollback or a flag) and I create a ticket for the proper fix with an owner and a date. Quick fixes without a follow-up become permanent technical debt. I tell the team clearly which one this is, so nobody thinks the shortcut is the final design.

### Q11. How do you decide when to refactor? (Senior signal)

I refactor when the old shape slows down work I need to do now, or when it causes bugs. I do it in small steps, with tests, near the code I am already changing. I avoid a big "rewrite everything" project unless there is a strong, measured reason, because rewrites take longer than expected and stop new features. I can explain the benefit in team terms: "This change makes the next three features about half the work."

### Q12. How do you review a pull request? (Must know)

First I read the description to understand the goal. Then I check the design (is this the right place and approach?), then correctness and edge cases, tests, security, and readability. I leave comments that explain why and offer an option. I mark small things as "nit" so the author knows they are optional. I aim to approve when the change makes the code better, even if it is not perfect. I reply quickly, because waiting is a cost for the author.

**Follow-up: "What do you do if the PR is too big?"** I ask the author to split it, or I ask for a short call to walk through it. Big PRs get shallow reviews, and bugs hide in them.

### Q13. How do you give feedback that is hard to hear? (Senior signal)

I do it soon, in private, and about a specific thing I saw, not about the person. I say the effect it had, and I ask what they think. Then we agree on one change to try. For example: "In yesterday's review the comments were short, and I think the new teammate felt shut down. Can we try adding a reason to each comment?" I follow up later and notice when it improves.

### Q14. How do you handle a junior who is slow? (Senior signal)

I first look for causes: unclear tasks, missing context, tasks that are too big, or a missing skill. I give smaller tasks with clear goals, do a short pairing session, and check in often at the start. I praise visible progress. If the problem continues after clear support, I talk to the manager. I try never to take over silently, because that teaches nothing and hides the real problem.

### Q15. How do you decide what to work on when everything is a priority? (Must know)

I ask the product owner or lead to rank them, since priorities are their call. I bring information to help: effort, risk, user impact, and what depends on what. I show a short list: "If I do A first, B moves a week. Which matters more?" I do not silently pick one and hide the others.

### Q16. A manager asks for a risky shortcut. What do you say? (Trap)

I do not just say yes or no. I explain the risk in business words: "If we skip this test, a bug in billing could charge people twice." Then I offer options: a smaller scope, a feature flag, a quick manual check, or a later date. If the choice is still to take the risk, I ask to write down that it was a decision, with a follow-up to pay it back. Real safety or legal problems (security, data protection) I do not trade away, and I say so politely and clearly.

### Q17. What is a decision you made that turned out wrong? (Must know, STAR)

Use a real story. A good one: you chose a tool or design for a reason that was fair at the time, you noticed the problem, you said so openly, you reduced the damage (migration, workaround), and you changed how you decide (for example, a short spike or a decision note before big choices). The trap: a "wrong" decision that is secretly a brag, or one where you blame someone else.

### Q18. How do you handle a production incident as the lead? (Senior signal)

First stop the damage (rollback, flag off, scale up). Name one person to coordinate and others to investigate, so people do not duplicate work or all talk at once. Send short updates on a schedule. After things are stable, find the causes without blaming a person, write a short postmortem, and create a few follow-up actions with owners and dates. Then I check later that the actions were really done.

### Q19. How do you know your team is doing well? (Senior signal)

I look at delivery measures (how often we ship, how long a change takes to reach users, how often releases fail, how fast we recover) and at things numbers cannot show: whether people feel safe to say "I don't know", whether reviews are quick, whether work is shared, and whether incidents teach us something. I use the measures to find process problems, not to rank people.

### Q20. What would you do in your first weeks as a team lead? (Senior signal)

Listen first. Meet each person one-to-one and ask what slows them down. Read the code and recent incidents to learn why things are the way they are. Find one or two quick wins that remove daily pain (a slow build, a flaky test). Agree on a few simple working rules with the team (review time, how we decide, how we write decision notes). I would not change many things at once, because trust is built by small, kept promises.

### Q21. How do you keep documentation and decisions from being lost? (Must know)

I write short decision records for choices that are hard to undo: the context, the decision, and the consequences (both good and bad). They live in the repository next to the code, so they change with it. I keep the README honest about how to run, test and deploy. I link the record from the pull request. When a decision is replaced, I mark the old one as superseded and point to the new one.

### Q22. How do you say "no" to a feature request? (Senior signal)

I say it with respect, a reason, and a way forward: "We can't ship this safely by Friday. The risk is X. We could ship a smaller version, or move the date. What helps you more?" I link it to the goal behind the request, because the request is sometimes a guess at a solution, and there may be a smaller way to reach the same goal.

### Q23. How do you stay up to date without chasing every new tool? (Must know)

I follow a few trusted sources, try new tools in small spikes on side projects or safe corners of the codebase, and ask "what problem does this solve for us?" before I recommend anything. I would rather know the basics deeply (the language, the browser, the database, HTTP) because they change slowly and make new tools easy to learn. I share what I learn with the team in short notes, and I say clearly when something is interesting but not ready for us.

### Q24. How do you make sure new people become productive quickly? (Senior signal)

I make sure the repository can be set up with a few commands, and the README says how. I prepare a first small task that touches the real release path. I give them a buddy, explain *why* things are built the way they are, and ask them to write down what confused them, then fix those docs. I check in often in the first weeks. A new person's questions are the best test of whether our documentation works.

## Key takeaways

- Treat every "why" as a chance to show your thinking, not as an attack.
- Use the six steps: **goal, options, choice, reason, risk, plan B**. Keep it to about 30 seconds.
- For a package: do you need it, is it maintained, does it fit, what does it cost, how hard is it to leave, and who can support it?
- For a language or framework: start from the need and the team. Prefer boring tools, and spend your "innovation" only where it pays off.
- "What are you thinking?" is answered with: goal, what I know, what I don't know, my guess, my next step.
- Give estimates as ranges with named unknowns, and update them early.
- Review for "better, not perfect", mark small comments as nits, and give reasons.
- Fix first, explain later. Be blameless about people, and strict about actions.
- Write down hard-to-reverse decisions in a short note. Future you and future teammates will thank you.
- Use team measures to find process problems, not to rank people.
- As a lead: give context, delegate with a clear owner, teach before rescuing, and say no with a reason and an option.
- All of this is common practice and opinion. Adapt it to your team, and always use your own true examples.

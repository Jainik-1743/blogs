> **In one line:** at four or more years, an interviewer is not checking whether you know the words. They want to hear **why** you choose something, **what it costs**, and **what breaks first**. This question bank is built to help you answer in that way.

## What changes at 4+ years

A junior question is "What is a hook?" A question for four years or more is "A page re-renders too often. How do you find out why, and how do you fix it without making the code harder to read?"

Interviewers listen for four things:

| They listen for | What a good answer sounds like |
|---|---|
| **A clear definition** | One or two plain sentences, no buzzwords. |
| **A trade-off** | "This is faster, but it costs memory." "This is simpler, but it does not scale past one server." |
| **A real example** | A small number, a bug you have seen, or a short piece of code. |
| **Honesty about limits** | "I would measure first." "I have not used that in production, but here is how I would test it." |

> [!ok]
> You do not need to know everything. You need to show that you can **reason**, **measure** and **say what you do not know**.

## How the questions are marked

Every lesson ends with an **Interview questions** section. Click a question to see a model answer, written the way you could say it out loud. Many answers carry one of three tags:

- **Must know:** almost every interviewer asks it. Get these right first.
- **Senior signal:** the question that separates a 2-year answer from a 4-year answer.
- **Trap:** a popular but wrong or out-of-date answer. Knowing the trap shows you have used the tool.

## How this bank was built

- Each lesson was written from the project's own documentation and source code, and from long-standing, widely used interview-question collections. Brand-new or thinly maintained sources were left out.
- Every code sample was **run or compiled** against the real packages (current stable versions at the time of writing). Where something could not be run, such as a database server or a hosted service, the lesson says so.
- Many popular interview answers online are **out of date**. Each lesson marks those traps. Here are the ones that surprise people most:

| Old answer people still give | What is true now |
|---|---|
| "Put scripts just before `</body>`." | Use `defer` in the head. |
| "`z-index: 9999` fixes stacking." | The cause is a stacking context. Fix that. |
| "Tailwind needs a `content` array and `tailwind.config.js`." | Tailwind v4 is CSS-first with `@theme` and finds files by itself. |
| "The React Compiler means you never need `useMemo`." | The compiler is a separate opt-in tool, and the hooks remain an escape hatch. |
| "`fetch` is cached by default in Next.js." | Not since Next.js 15. In 16 you opt in with `use cache`. |
| "`middleware.ts` handles requests." | It is `proxy.ts` now, on the Node.js runtime. |
| "`npm i prisma` gives the stable version." | It can install a release candidate. Pin the version. |
| "`prisma.$use` middleware." | Removed. Use client extensions. |
| "`nextTick` always runs first." | In an ES module, a resolved promise runs first. |
| "`CMD [\"npm\",\"start\"]` is fine in Docker." | It can swallow `SIGTERM`, so shutdown never runs. |
| "`alias()` links Mixpanel users." | That is the legacy way. Use `identify` with simplified ID merge. |
| "Temperature 0 means the same answer every time." | Not in practice. |
| "AI agent and agentic AI are two defined terms." | There is no standard definition. Say what you mean. |

## A two-week plan

1. **Days 1–3:** HTML & CSS, Tailwind, TypeScript. Do the "must know" questions out loud.
2. **Days 4–6:** React and Next.js. Re-read the traps, then rebuild one small example from memory.
3. **Days 7–9:** Node.js, MongoDB, Prisma and SQL, Hasura and GraphQL.
4. **Days 10–11:** LLMs, agents and RAG, then the AI SDK. Be ready to explain the two meanings of "harness".
5. **Day 12:** Meilisearch, Mixpanel and the library lesson.
6. **Days 13–14:** The experience round. Say three stories out loud with real numbers, then do two mock interviews with a friend.

## Interview questions

### Q1. What do interviewers look for at four years that they do not look for at two?
They look for **judgement**. At two years it is enough to know how a tool works. At four years they want to know when you would **not** use it, what it costs, and how you would find out if it was the wrong choice. I try to answer with a definition, a trade-off and a short example, in that order.

### Q2. A question has several correct answers. How do you pick one?
I first ask one or two **clarifying questions**: how big is the data, how many users, what is the deadline. Then I give the simple option and say why it is enough for now. Then I say what would make me change it. That shows I do not over-build and that I know the next step.

### Q3. You do not know the answer. What do you do?
I say so, plainly, and then I reason from what I do know. For example: "I have not used that library, but it sounds like it solves the same problem as X. I would check the docs for how it handles failure, and I would test it with a small example first." Honest reasoning scores higher than a confident guess that turns out wrong.

### Q4. How do you show experience without a long story?
I use numbers and one concrete detail. "The page loaded in 4.2 seconds. I found a 600 KB library used in one place, loaded it lazily, and got it to 2.1 seconds." The numbers make it believable, and the detail shows I did the work myself.

### Q5. Should you memorise the answers in this bank?
No. Read the definition and the trade-off, then close the page and explain it in your own words. If you can explain **why** the trap is a trap, you will handle a version of the question you have never seen.

### Q6. How should you use the "trap" questions?
As a checklist of things that have changed. If you worked with an older version, say so: "Before version 15, `fetch` was cached by default. Now you opt in." That shows you have used the tool for a long time **and** kept up with it.

### Q7. What if the interviewer asks about a tool you have never used?
I connect it to something I know, and I say that I am doing so. "Meilisearch is a search engine, so I would think about it like Elasticsearch: indexes, documents, ranking. The main thing I would check is how it handles typos and how to keep it in sync with the database." Then I ask what they use it for.

### Q8. How do you end an answer well?
With one sentence about **how you would check it**. "I would confirm it with the profiler." "I would add a test that fails without the fix." "I would watch the error rate for a day." It tells the interviewer that you treat your own answers as claims to prove.

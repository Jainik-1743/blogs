> **In one line:** a system design interview is a **spoken performance with a drawing**, not a quiz. You keep to a clock, ask before you build, draw a few boxes, say one trade-off in one sentence, use rough numbers to decide, and stay calm when the interviewer says "what if this fails?".

Most people who fail a system design round do not fail because they lack knowledge. They fail because they talk without a plan, draw twenty boxes in the first five minutes, never say a number, or go silent when asked about a failure. This lesson is about **what to say, and when**.

It is not a new theory lesson. The theory is already in other lessons, and I link to it instead of repeating it:

## Words used in this lesson

- **A mock interview** is a practice interview with a friend or a timer. You do the real thing out loud, so mistakes happen in practice and not in the real room.
- **Functional requirements** say *what* the system does ("create a short link, redirect from it").
- **Non-functional requirements** say *how well* it must do it (fast, available, durable, safe).
- **Scale numbers** are the rough sizes: users, requests per second, data per year.
- **QPS (queries per second)** is the number of requests in one second. **Peak** is the busiest moment, usually a few times the average.
- **Back-of-the-envelope** means rough maths done quickly, with round numbers, to decide a design.
- **An API sketch** is a short list of calls (method, path, input, output) without full detail.
- **A trade-off** is a choice where you win one thing and lose another. Fast and simple often cost something else.
- **A single point of failure (SPOF)** is one part whose failure stops the whole system.
- **Failover** means switching to a spare when the main part fails.
- **A deep dive** is the part of the interview where you go into one area in detail.
- **Think aloud** means saying your thoughts as you have them, so the interviewer can follow and help.

## The 45-minute timeline

A 45-minute round has about 40 minutes of real design time. The rest is hello and goodbye. This is the clock I practise with. Keep a small clock or timer in view.

| Time | Phase | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| 0 to 5 | **Requirements** | Ask questions. Agree the scope. Say the non-functional needs. |
| 5 to 10 | **Numbers and API** | Rough scale, storage, a short API sketch. |
| 10 to 20 | **High-level boxes** | Draw the main path: client, service, store, cache. Walk one request through. |
| 20 to 35 | **Deep dive** | Pick the hardest part (here: making unique codes and fast redirects). Go deep. |
| 35 to 42 | **Failure and scale** | "What if X fails?" "What if traffic is 10 times bigger?" Name trade-offs. |
| 42 to 45 | **Wrap-up** | Summarise in four sentences. Name what you would do next. |

Two rules about the clock:

1. **Say the plan once, at the start.** "I will take about five minutes for requirements, then boxes, then I would like to go deep on code generation and the redirect path, and keep five minutes for failures." Now the interviewer knows your plan, and they can steer you early.
2. **Never spend more than ten minutes on the first drawing.** The most common time trap is a beautiful first diagram and no time for the hard part.

> [!warn] If the interviewer interrupts and takes you somewhere else, **go with them**. The clock is yours, but the interviewer chooses what they want to see. Say "Sure, let us go there, and I will come back to the cache later," then really come back.

This is the same shape as the five steps in [Lesson 59](/system-design/lesson-59) of the system design series, and the six-step frame in [Design problems, Lesson 0](/design-problems/lesson-0). The names differ a little. The idea is the same: **clarify, size, sketch, deepen, break, summarise**.

## The first five minutes: requirements, scale, API

The first five minutes decide the mood. Do three things, in this order.

### Step 1. Repeat the problem in your own words

"So we are building a service that takes a long URL and gives back a short one, and when someone opens the short one, we send them to the long one. Is that right?"

This takes ten seconds. It proves you listened, and it catches a wrong guess early.

### Step 2. Ask five or six scoping questions

Ask short, closed questions. Each should change the design. Do not ask 20 questions. Do not ask questions whose answer would not change what you draw.

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| "Do users need custom aliases, like `/summer-sale`?" | Changes the code generation |
| "Do links expire?" | Changes storage and cleanup |
| "Do we need click counts?" | Changes the redirect path (analytics) |
| "Roughly how many new links per month, and how many clicks?" | Gives you scale numbers |
| "Is it fine if a click is slow once in a while, or is it always fast?" | Gives you the latency goal |
| "Is this one region or global?" | Changes the deploy plan |

When the interviewer says "you decide", **decide out loud and write it down**: "I will assume 100 million new links a month and 100 reads for each write. I will write that on the board. Tell me if you want different numbers."

> [!note] "You decide" is not a trick. It is a test of whether you can set sensible assumptions and move on. A clear assumption that you state is better than a long wait for the perfect number.

### Step 3. Say the non-functional needs in one breath

"For quality I care about four things: redirects must be fast, the service must be highly available, codes must be unique, and a saved link must not be lost. I accept that analytics can be a little late."

That last sentence is a trade-off you made *before* the interviewer asked. It shows good judgement.

### Step 4. Sketch the API in two lines each

Say the call, the input and the output. Do not describe every field.

```text
POST /links   { "longUrl": "...", "alias"?: "...", "expiresAt"?: "..." }   ->  201 { "code": "aZ3k9Qp" }
GET  /{code}                                                                ->  302 Location: <longUrl>   (or 404 / 410)
```

Say: "I will use `302` so the browser asks us each time. That lets us count clicks and turn off bad links. `301` would be cheaper, but the browser would cache it." (This choice is explained in [Design problems, Lesson 1](/design-problems/lesson-1).)

**Rule of thumb:** after five minutes, there should be four things on the board: **requirements, numbers, API, and the question you will go deep on**.

## How to draw boxes

You are not an artist. You are making a map that the interviewer can follow while you speak.

1. **Draw left to right, in the order a request travels.** Client, load balancer, app servers, cache, database. If you draw it in the order of the story, you can point and tell the story.
2. **Start with the smallest working picture.** Client, one service, one database. Walk one request through. Only then add parts, and say **why** you add each one.
3. **Name each box with a job, not a product.** Write "cache" and "database", and *say* "Redis" and "Postgres" if you like. The picture should still make sense if you change the product.
4. **Draw arrows with a short label.** "GET /abc", "read", "async event". An arrow with no words hides the important thing: what travels on it.
5. **Use two line styles.** A solid arrow for the request the user waits for. A dashed arrow for background work (analytics, cleanup). This one habit answers many questions before they are asked.
6. **Number the steps of the main request.** Write 1, 2, 3 next to the arrows and say them as you point.
7. **Leave space.** You will add a replica, a queue or a second region later.

A good first picture for our problem has only five boxes:

```text
 client --1--> [ load balancer ] --2--> [ app servers (stateless) ] --3--> [ cache ] --4 (miss)--> [ database ]
                                                 |
                                                 +- - - - async - - - -> [ queue ] - - -> [ analytics store ]
```

Say it like this: "1: the click hits a load balancer. 2: it goes to any app server. The servers keep no state, so I can add more. 3: the server looks in the cache. 4: only on a miss, it reads the database. The dashed path is click counting. It does not block the redirect."

> [!warn] **Do not draw the final picture first.** If your first drawing has a queue, three caches, a CDN and two databases, the interviewer cannot tell *why* any of them are there. A picture that grows one step at a time shows your thinking. A finished picture shows only your memory.

## How to state a trade-off in one sentence

A trade-off sentence has four parts: **I choose A, because of B, and I give up C, which is fine because D.**

> "I choose a cache in front of the database, because reads are 100 times more than writes, and I give up some freshness, which is fine because a link rarely changes."

Here are more with the same shape:

| Decision | One-sentence version |
|---|---|
| Random codes vs a counter | "I choose random 7-character codes because they need no shared state, and I give up a guaranteed first-try insert, which is fine because a collision is very rare and I retry." |
| 302 vs 301 | "I choose 302 because I want click counts and a way to turn links off, and I give up some extra load, which the cache can take." |
| SQL vs NoSQL | "I choose a key-value style store because the only query is by code, and I give up joins, which we do not need." |
| Async analytics | "I count clicks through a queue, so the redirect stays fast, and I give up exact real-time counts, which is fine for a dashboard." |

Three habits make these sentences strong:

- **Say what you give up.** If you only list the benefits, it sounds like you did not think.
- **Tie the reason to a number or a requirement you already said.** "Because reads are 100 times more" is a reason. "Because it is better" is not.
- **Stop after the sentence.** Do not defend it for two minutes. If the interviewer wants more, they will ask. Then you go deeper.

## How to use numbers

You do not need exact answers. You need **round numbers, shown work, and a decision at the end**. The full method is in [Back-of-the-Envelope Estimation](/system-design/lesson-10); here is how to *say* it.

**Rules for speaking numbers:**

1. **Round hard.** A month has about 2.5 million seconds (30 days times 86,400, which is 2.59 million). Say "about 2.5 million" and move on.
2. **Say the unit every time.** "40 writes per second", "3 terabytes", not "40" or "3".
3. **Show the line, then the answer.** "100 million a month, divided by 2.5 million seconds, is about 40 per second." The interviewer can check you.
4. **Ask "so what?" after every number.** 40 writes per second is small: one database handles that. 4,000 reads per second is more, so a cache helps. The number is only useful if it changes a choice.
5. **Peak is about 5 times the average.** Say that you assume it. Design for peak, not average.
6. **If you are off by 2 times, nobody cares. If you are off by 100 times, say it again.** Check the size of the answer: "3 terabytes. Does that sound right? 6 billion rows at half a kilobyte each. Yes."

**A pattern I use to open the numbers:** "Let me do two quick numbers: traffic and storage. Then I will decide if one machine is enough."

> [!note] Never skip the "so what". A list of numbers with no decision looks like homework. The interviewer wants to see you **use** the number: "40 writes a second means a single primary database is fine, so I will not shard at this size."

## How to handle "what if X fails?"

This question is not an attack. It is the main test. Interviewers ask it to see if you think about **failure as normal**. A calm, repeatable answer beats a clever one.

Use four steps. I call it **detect, limit, recover, accept**.

1. **Detect:** how do we know it failed? (health checks, timeouts, alerts)
2. **Limit:** how do we stop the damage from spreading? (timeouts, retries with a limit, circuit breakers, falling back to a degraded mode)
3. **Recover:** how does it come back? (a replica takes over, a new instance starts, we replay from a queue)
4. **Accept:** what do users see in the meantime, and is that acceptable? (slower, slightly old data, or a clear error)

Example, in speech: "If the cache goes down: we detect it with a timeout on the cache call. We limit damage by falling back to the database, with a short timeout, so a slow cache cannot block the request. Reads go up by 100 times on the database for a while, so I would also have a limit on connections, and I would warm the cache again slowly after it returns. Users see slightly slower redirects, not errors. I accept that for a short time."

| If this fails | A calm first sentence |
|---|---|
| Cache | "Reads fall through to the database. I protect it with a connection limit and warm the cache slowly." |
| One app server | "It is stateless, so the load balancer stops sending traffic after a failed health check. Others take over." |
| Database primary | "A replica is promoted. We may lose the last few writes, depending on how replication is set. For links, I would accept a short pause in creating links, but not in redirects, which read from replicas." |
| Queue or analytics | "Redirects do not depend on it. Clicks wait in the queue or are dropped for a short time. I would accept a small undercount." |
| Whole region | "For this answer I would run in two regions with traffic split by DNS, and I would say the cost and the data sync problem out loud." |

Rules for this part:

- **Do not say "it will never fail".** Everything fails.
- **Do not add three new boxes in a panic.** Often the right answer is "I accept it", with a reason.
- **Separate the paths.** Say which path the user waits for (redirect) and which can be late (analytics). This is the strongest sentence in a failure answer.
- **If you do not know, say how you would find out.** "I do not remember the exact failover time of that product. I would read its docs, and test it in a drill."

## Phrases to use, and phrases to avoid

Words carry the interview. Use these.

**Phrases to use**

| Moment | Say |
|---|---|
| Start | "Before I design anything, may I ask a few questions?" |
| Plan | "I will take about five minutes for requirements, then boxes, then a deep dive." |
| Assumption | "I will assume X. Tell me if that is wrong." |
| Number | "Roughly, about... so what that means for us is..." |
| Choice | "I choose A over B because..., and what I give up is..." |
| Unsure | "I am not sure. My best guess is X, and I would check by doing Y." |
| Checking in | "Does this level of detail work, or should I go deeper here?" |
| Hint received | "Good point. That changes this part. Let me update the picture." |
| Failure | "If that fails, we detect it by..., we limit it by..., and users see..." |
| Wrap-up | "To summarise: ... The weakest part of my design is..., and I would fix it next by..." |

**Phrases to avoid**

| Avoid | Why | Say instead |
|---|---|---|
| "It depends." (and stop) | A pause with no content | "It depends on read volume. At our 4,000 a second I would..." |
| "We just use Kafka / microservices / Kubernetes." | A product name with no reason | "I would add a queue here, because..." |
| "That is easy." | Hides your thinking, and it may be wrong | Walk through it |
| "Never" and "always" | Rarely true in design | "In most cases", "at this scale" |
| "Google/Netflix does it this way." | You are not Google | "At our size, a simpler version is enough." |
| "I would use a NoSQL database because it scales." | Too thin | "The only query is by code, so a key-value store fits." |
| "Sorry, I am bad at this." | Spends your confidence | Say nothing, or "Let me think for a moment." |
| Long silence | The interviewer cannot help or score you | "I am thinking about X versus Y." |
| Arguing with a hint | Looks fixed and defensive | "I see. Let me try that." |

> [!ok] It is fine to pause. Say "give me a moment to think" and take ten seconds. A short quiet pause that you announce looks calm. A silence that you do not explain looks lost.

## A worked walkthrough: design a short-link service

Below is a **transcript-style** answer. "I" is you. The interviewer's lines are short. Times are minutes:seconds from the start. The design matches [Design problems, Lesson 1](/design-problems/lesson-1), so read that for the full theory and code. Here we only practise *speaking*.

> [!note] The numbers are examples I chose for the practice. Real interviewers will give their own. A real answer would also adjust to what the interviewer says. Treat this as a rhythm to copy, not a script to learn by heart.

### 0:00 to 0:05, requirements

**Interviewer:** "Design a URL shortener."

**Me (0:00):** "Sure. So the service takes a long URL and returns a short link, and when someone opens the short link, we redirect them to the long URL. Is that right?"

**Interviewer:** "Yes."

**Me (0:20):** "May I ask a few questions? First, do users choose their own alias, like `/summer-sale`?"

**Interviewer:** "Nice to have. Skip it for now."

**Me (0:30):** "OK, I will note it as out of scope and come back if there is time. Do links expire, and do we count clicks?"

**Interviewer:** "Links can have an optional expiry. Click counts are a nice feature."

**Me (0:45):** "Good. How big should I assume it is?"

**Interviewer:** "You decide."

**Me (0:50):** "I will assume 100 million new links per month, and that people click about 100 times for every link created. I will write that down. For quality, I care about four things: redirects must be fast, in tens of milliseconds, and highly available; codes must be unique; saved links must not be lost; and I accept that click counts can be a little late. Is that a fair start?"

**Interviewer:** "Fair."

**Me (1:30):** "Here is my plan for the 45 minutes. A few minutes on numbers and the API, then a first drawing, then a deep dive on how we make unique codes and keep redirects fast. I would like to keep the last ten minutes for failures and scale."

*(What this showed: restate, scope, assume out loud, state the quality goals and a trade-off, say the plan. About ninety seconds.)*

### 1:30 to 10:00, numbers and API

**Me (1:40):** "Quick numbers. Writes first. 100 million a month divided by about 2.5 million seconds in a month is about 40 writes per second. Peak, say 5 times, is about 200. That is small. One database can take that."

"Reads are 100 times more: about 4,000 a second, and about 20,000 at peak. **That** is the number that shapes the design. It tells me reads must come from a cache, and the app servers must be easy to add."

"Storage. If we keep links for 5 years: 100 million times 12 months times 5 is 6 billion links. At about 500 bytes each, that is 3 terabytes. That is too much for a tiny server, but fine for a normal database with replicas. Write speed is not the problem here. Size is the thing to watch."

"Code length. If I use letters and digits, 62 choices per character, 7 characters give 62 to the power 7, which is about 3.5 trillion codes. We need 6 billion, so we use under 1 percent of the space. That makes random codes safe."

**Me (5:00):** "The API, in two calls." *(writes on the board)*

```text
POST /links  { longUrl, expiresAt? }  ->  201 { code }
GET  /{code}                          ->  302 Location: longUrl   (404 unknown, 410 expired)
```

"I use 302, so the browser asks us on every click. That costs more traffic, but gives us click counts and a way to turn off bad links. A 301 is cheaper because browsers remember it, but we lose both."

**Interviewer:** "Why not just 301 and count in the browser?"

**Me (6:30):** "Good question. Counting in the browser would need a script on the target page, and we do not control that page. So the server must see each click, which means 302. If analytics did not matter, I would use 301 and save load."

*(What this showed: every number ends in a decision. The interviewer's challenge was answered with the reason.)*

### 10:00 to 20:00, boxes

**Me (10:00):** "Now I will draw the smallest version." *(draws: client, app server, database)* "The client sends a POST to the app server. The server makes a code, saves `code -> longUrl` in the database, and returns the code. The client opens the short link, the server looks up the code, and answers 302."

"Reads are 4,000 a second, so I add a cache between the server and the database." *(draws cache, solid arrow)* "The server checks the cache first. On a miss it reads the database and fills the cache. Because links almost never change, a cache with a long lifetime works well. If I estimate a hot set of about 10 gigabytes, it fits in one cache node with a spare."

"I put a load balancer in front, and I keep the app servers stateless, so any server can answer any request." *(draws load balancer)*

"One more thing, shown dashed: click counting." *(draws a dashed arrow to a queue, then to an analytics store)* "The server puts a small click event on a queue and returns the redirect at once. A worker adds up the counts later. The user never waits for it."

**Interviewer:** "Why not update a counter in the database at each click?"

**Me (15:30):** "At 20,000 peak clicks a second, that is 20,000 writes on a hot row, which would be the slowest part of the system. A queue lets me batch them. The cost is that counts are a few seconds late, and I said earlier that is acceptable."

**Me (18:00):** "Let me walk one click through the picture." *(points along the numbered arrows)* "Load balancer, app server, cache hit, 302. About one or two milliseconds inside our system. On a miss, one database read, then the cache is filled."

*(What this showed: smallest picture first, each box added for a number-based reason, a dashed line for background work, a walk-through at the end.)*

### 20:00 to 35:00, deep dive

**Interviewer:** "Let's go deep on creating codes. How do you make a unique one?"

**Me (20:10):** "I see three options. One: hash the long URL and cut it. Two: a global counter turned into letters. Three: random codes with a retry. I will explain each in a sentence, then choose."

"Hash and cut: simple, but two different URLs can give the same prefix, and the same URL always gives the same code, which is a problem if two users want separate links. Counter: no collisions ever, but one counter is a single point of failure, and sequential codes are easy to guess and scrape. Random: no shared state, and the chance of a clash is tiny."

"**I choose random 7-character codes, and I give up a guaranteed first-try insert, which is fine because it is rare.** The database has the code as the primary key. If the insert fails because the code exists, I make a new code and try again. With 6 billion used out of 3.5 trillion, about 0.17 percent of tries clash at the very end. So the average is about 1.002 tries."

**Interviewer:** "What if two servers make the same code at the same time?"

**Me (24:00):** "The primary key makes one insert win and the other fail. The loser retries with a new code. So uniqueness is enforced by the database, not by the app servers. That is why I trust it."

**Interviewer:** "Now the redirect path. 20,000 reads a second at peak. Walk me through the cache."

**Me (27:00):** "I use cache-aside: look in the cache, on a miss read the database, then store the result with a time limit. For popular links, hit rate should be very high, because a small share of links gets most of the clicks. I'd measure the hit rate from day one. If it was low, I'd look for a wrong time limit or a cache that is too small."

"Two details. First, **expiry**: if a link has an expiry date, I store the time with it, and the redirect checks it, so a cached link still stops at the right time. Second, **deletion**: if we turn off a bad link, we must remove it from the cache too, or it keeps working. I do that on the write path, with a short cache lifetime as a safety net."

**Interviewer:** "And a stampede? A link goes viral just as its cache entry expires."

**Me (31:00):** "Good one. Thousands of requests miss at once and hit the database together. I would add a short lock per code, so one request reloads it while the others wait a moment, or serve the old value a little longer while one request refreshes it. I would also add a random amount to the time limit, so entries do not all expire together."

*(What this showed: options listed, one chosen with a one-sentence trade-off, uniqueness placed in the database, details like expiry and deletion, and a clear answer to a follow-up with an unknown topic by naming the problem and two fixes.)*

### 35:00 to 42:00, failures and scale

**Interviewer:** "What if the cache goes down?"

**Me (35:00):** "We detect it with a short timeout on cache calls and a health check. We limit the damage by falling back to the database. The database then gets up to the full 4,000 to 20,000 reads a second, so I have a connection limit and a small local cache in each app server for the hottest links. We recover by bringing the cache back and warming it slowly. Users see slower redirects but not errors. I accept that for a short time."

**Interviewer:** "What if the database primary dies?"

**Me (37:30):** "Reads come from replicas, so redirects continue. A replica is promoted to primary. In that time, creating links is paused or fails with a clear error, and with asynchronous replication we may lose the last few writes. For short links I'd accept a short pause in *creating* links, but not in *redirecting*. If losing even one link is not acceptable, I'd use synchronous replication for that and say we pay in write latency."

**Interviewer:** "What if traffic grows 10 times?"

**Me (40:00):** "Redirect traffic scales by adding app servers and cache nodes, because they are stateless or hold copies. The first real limit would be database size, not speed: 30 terabytes. At that point I'd split the links table by code into shards, because every lookup is by code. I would not do it today, at 3 terabytes, because it adds work with no benefit."

### 42:00 to 45:00, wrap-up

**Me (42:10):** "To summarise. The main path is load balancer, stateless servers, cache, database. Codes are random, 7 characters, made unique by the primary key. Redirects use 302 so we can count and disable links. Clicks go through a queue, so they never slow the redirect. The weakest part of my design is the cache: if it fails, the database takes a big load. Next I would add the connection limit and a drill that turns the cache off on purpose. I did not cover custom aliases, abuse protection or multi-region. I'd start with a block-list check on create."

**Interviewer:** "Thanks, that was clear."

> [!ok] Count what happened. In 45 minutes the candidate asked questions, gave numbers with a decision after each, drew five boxes, gave three one-sentence trade-offs, answered four failure questions in the same pattern, and ended with the weakest point. None of this needs special genius. It needs practice.

## Self-scoring checklist

After each mock, give yourself one point for each "yes". This is my own list, not an official scoring sheet. Do the same drill in small parts first (only the first five minutes, only the numbers, only one drawing), then run a full 45 minutes with a friend or a timer, and record it.

**Start.** I repeated the problem. I asked 4 to 6 questions that changed the design. I said my assumptions out loud and wrote them down. I stated the quality goals (speed, availability, durability). I sketched the API. I said my time plan once. (6 points)

**Numbers.** I computed writes per second, reads per second and storage. I said units every time. Every number ended with a decision. I named a peak and how I got it. (4 points)

**Drawing.** I started with the smallest picture. I added each box with a reason. Every arrow had a label. I separated the user path from background work. I walked one request through. (5 points)

**Trade-offs.** I gave at least three, in one sentence each, and each said what I give up. I did not defend a choice for more than a minute. (3 points)

**Failures.** I answered "what if X fails?" with detect, limit, recover, accept. I said what the user sees. I did not claim anything never fails. (3 points)

**Talking.** No silence over ten seconds without saying what I was thinking. I used a hint instead of arguing with it. I checked the depth once or twice. I ended with a summary and my own weakest point. (4 points)

**Score out of 25:** 20 or more is strong. 14 to 19 is good: find the section where you lost points and repeat that drill. Below 14: do the first-five-minutes drill again before you try a full mock. When you listen to the recording, also count filler words and numbers that had no decision after them.

## Interview questions

These are questions about the *interview itself* and how to talk through it. The answers are first person, and you should adapt them to your own style.

### Q1. How do you start a system design interview? (Must know)

I do not draw anything in the first minute. I repeat the problem in my own words to check I understood. Then I ask four to six short questions that change the design: features in scope, expected scale, latency and availability needs, and whether it is one region or global. When I get "you decide", I choose a number, say it out loud and write it down. Then I say my time plan once. This takes about five minutes and it gives me a clear scope that I can point to later when I make choices.

### Q2. How many clarifying questions are too many? (Must know)

When the answer would not change what I draw, the question is too much. Five or six good questions are enough. I avoid asking about colours, login screens or small details that are not part of the core. I also avoid a list of twenty questions in a row, because it feels like I am avoiding the problem. If I am unsure, I make an assumption, say it, and move on. The interviewer can correct me.

### Q3. The interviewer says "you decide the scale". What do I do? (Must know)

I choose round numbers that fit the product, say them out loud, and write them on the board. For example, "100 million new links a month and 100 reads per write". Then I check them against common sense. If the interviewer wants other numbers, they will say so. It is a test of judgement, not of memory. The wrong move is to wait for the perfect number or to refuse to answer.

### Q4. How do you split the 45 minutes? (Must know)

I use about five minutes for requirements, five for numbers and API, ten for the first drawing, fifteen for a deep dive, seven for failures and scale, and three to summarise. I tell the interviewer this plan at the start, so they can steer me. The numbers are a guide. If the interviewer wants to spend 20 minutes on one area, I follow them and shorten something else. What I never do is spend 25 minutes on the first drawing.

### Q5. How should I draw the diagram? (Must know)

I draw the smallest working version first, left to right, in the order a request travels. Then I add parts one at a time and say why I add each one. I label every arrow with what travels on it. I use solid lines for what the user waits for and dashed lines for background work. I walk one request through with numbered steps. I name boxes by their job ("cache", "queue") and say the product name only if it helps. I leave space for later changes.

### Q6. How do you state a trade-off in one sentence? (Must know)

I use this shape: "I choose A because B, and I give up C, which is fine because D." For example: "I choose a cache in front of the database because reads are 100 times more than writes, and I give up some freshness, which is fine because links rarely change." The reason must tie to a number or a requirement I already stated. I stop after the sentence, and go deeper only if asked.

### Q7. What if I do not know the answer to a technical question? (Must know)

I say so, calmly, and show how I would think. "I do not remember the exact limit of that product. My best guess is around X, and I would confirm it in the docs and with a small test before I rely on it." Then I continue with the design using my guess as an assumption. Interviewers often respect honesty more than a confident wrong claim. What I never do is make up a number and defend it.

### Q8. How do I use numbers without wasting time? (Must know)

I do two or three quick calculations: traffic, storage, and sometimes cache size or bandwidth. I round hard (a month is about 2.5 million seconds), say units, and show the line of maths. After each result I say what it means for the design, such as "40 writes per second means one primary database is enough". I use a peak of about five times the average and say that I assume it. I keep it under five minutes. A number with no decision after it is wasted time. See [Back-of-the-Envelope Estimation](/system-design/lesson-10) for the method.

### Q9. Walk me through what you say when asked "what if the cache fails?" (Must know)

I use four steps: detect, limit, recover, accept. "We detect it with a timeout on cache calls and a health check. We limit the damage by falling back to the database, with a connection limit so it is not overloaded, and a small in-process cache for the hottest items. We recover by restarting or failing over the cache and warming it slowly. Users see slower redirects, not errors, and I accept that for a short time." The key is the last step: say what the user sees and whether it is acceptable.

### Q10. What if the interviewer asks about a failure you have not thought about? (Senior signal)

I stop and name the path. "Let me think about what depends on this part. The redirect does not, but the click counting does." Then I use the same four steps. I try to separate the path the user waits for from the path that can be late, since that often makes the failure small. If I find a real weak spot, I say so: "That is a single point of failure in my design. I would fix it by adding a second instance or by removing the dependency." Admitting a weak spot is better than hiding it.

### Q11. The interviewer gives a hint that changes my design. How do I react? (Must know)

I say "Good point" and show I understood it, then update the picture and say what changes. "If clicks must be exact, then the queue is not enough. I would count in the database in batches, and the trade-off is more load." I do not argue or say "I already thought about that". Hints are a sign the interviewer wants me to succeed. Taking a hint and using it well is a positive signal.

### Q12. How do I handle a long silence in my own head? (Must know)

I say what I am doing. "Let me think for a moment about whether to use a counter or random codes." If I am really stuck, I go back to a simpler version: "A simple version is this. Let me solve it first, and then improve it." Or I say the options I can see, even if I cannot choose yet. Ten seconds of announced thinking is fine. A long silence that I do not explain leaves the interviewer with nothing to score.

### Q13. How do I deep dive well? (Senior signal)

I pick the hardest part, or the part the interviewer chooses. I list two or three options in a sentence each, choose one, and give a one-sentence trade-off. Then I go into details a real system needs: what the data looks like, what happens on a collision or a retry, how I handle expiry and deletion, and what I measure. I try to name one failure of my own choice. For a short-link service, that was code generation and cache stampedes. Depth means going one level below the box, not drawing more boxes.

### Q14. What do I say at the end? (Must know)

A short summary in four or five sentences: the main path, the two or three key choices, and the weakest point. Then what I would do next, and what I left out. "I did not cover custom aliases, abuse protection or multi-region. I would start with a block-list check." Ending with my own weakness shows honesty and judgement, and it often leads to a good last question from the interviewer.

### Q15. How do I practise by myself? (Must know)

I practise out loud, with a timer, and I record myself. First I drill small parts: the first five minutes, the numbers, one drawing, one-sentence trade-offs, failure answers. Then I do a full 45 minutes with a friend. When I listen to the recording, I count silences longer than ten seconds, filler words, and numbers that had no decision after them. I use problems from [Design problems](/design-problems) and compare my answer with the written lesson afterwards, not before.

### Q16. What is the single biggest mistake, and how do I avoid it? (Trap)

Starting to draw or to name products before I understand the problem and the scale. The cure is a fixed opening: repeat the problem, ask the questions, state the assumptions and goals, do two quick numbers, and write the API. After that, every box has a reason. The second biggest mistake is silence, and the cure is to think aloud. Both are habits, so they improve with practice, not with more reading.

## Key takeaways

- Keep to a clock: about 5 minutes of requirements, 5 of numbers and API, 10 of boxes, 15 of deep dive, 7 of failures, 3 to summarise. Say the plan once.
- Open by repeating the problem, asking 4 to 6 useful questions, stating assumptions out loud, and sketching the API.
- Draw the smallest picture first, left to right, add parts one by one with a reason, and label every arrow. Show background work with dashed lines.
- Say each trade-off in one sentence: I choose A because B, I give up C, which is fine because D.
- Use round numbers, say units, show the line of maths, and always end with a decision.
- For "what if X fails?" use detect, limit, recover, accept, and say what the user sees.
- Say "I am not sure, here is how I would check" instead of guessing. Take hints gladly. Never go silent without saying what you are thinking.
- End with a short summary and your own weakest point.
- Practise out loud, in small drills first, then full mocks, and score yourself with the checklist.

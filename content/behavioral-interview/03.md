> **In one line:** ownership, leadership and impact stories show your **level**. Juniors show they finish what they start. Seniors show they see problems nobody assigned, make decisions with missing facts, bring other people along, and can say **what changed because of them, with a number**.

The stories in [lesson 2](/behavioral-interview/lesson-2) show how you behave when things go wrong. These stories show what you do when things are going right, or when nobody has told you what to do. Interviewers use them to decide your level. Two people can both say "I led the migration." One means "I did the tickets my lead gave me." The other means "I found the problem, wrote the proposal, convinced three teams, and measured the result." The words are the same. The stories are not.

This lesson covers five story types: **ownership without a title, leading a project, mentoring, deciding with missing information, and measuring your impact**. For each one you get a shape, a weak and a good spoken answer side by side, and the reason. At the end there is a worksheet for finding the numbers in your own work, even if you never kept any.

## Words used in this lesson

- **Ownership** means treating a problem as yours until it is solved, even if nobody gave it to you.
- **Initiative** is starting useful work without waiting to be asked.
- **Scope** is the size of what you handled: one task, one feature, a service, a team, several teams.
- **Influence without authority** means getting people to act when you are not their manager.
- **Impact** is the change in the world because of your work: time saved, money earned, errors reduced.
- **Baseline** is the "before" number you compare against.
- **Proxy metric** is a number that stands in for the thing you can't measure directly, such as support tickets for "user confusion."
- **Mentoring** means helping another person grow over time, not only answering a single question.
- **Reversible decision** is a choice you can undo cheaply. An irreversible one is expensive to undo.
- **Stakeholder** is anyone affected by or interested in your work, such as users, support, sales or a manager.
- **Proposal** (or RFC, design doc) is a short written plan for a change that people can comment on before work starts.

## Why the same story sounds junior or senior

Look at these three sentences about the same event:

1. "I was asked to update the build script, and I did it." (Task.)
2. "I noticed our builds were slow, so I looked into why and fixed the main cause." (Problem.)
3. "Builds were costing the team about two hours a day. I proposed a plan, got two teams to agree, led the change, and cut build time by 60 percent." (System.)

All are true stories about builds. Their **scope** is different:

| Level | What the story shows |
| --- | --- |
| Junior | You complete tasks well, ask good questions, and learn quickly |
| Mid | You own a feature or service from start to end and handle problems in it |
| Senior | You find the right problems, make design decisions, bring others along and reduce risk |
| Staff and above | You shape direction across teams and make other people better |

Most candidates tell stories one level below where they work. The fix is not to exaggerate. It is to **pull out** the decisions and the influence that are already in your real stories.

## Type 1: Ownership without a title

### What the interviewer wants

Real questions: "Give an example of a time you took initiative." "What is something that you had to push for in your previous projects?" "Have you ever worked on something without getting approval from your manager?" "Tell me about a time you saw a problem and fixed it though it wasn't your job."

They are looking for:

1. You **notice** problems.
2. You **act**, but you stay within safe limits (you don't break rules or surprise people).
3. You **follow through** until the thing is actually fixed.
4. You **check** that it worked.

The question about working without approval is a trap in the good sense: the right answer is not "I did what I wanted." It is "I acted on a small, safe, reversible thing, and I told my manager promptly."

### The shape: notice, decide, act, tell, check

1. **What you noticed** and why it mattered (with a cost).
2. **Why it was nobody's job**, or why nobody had done it.
3. **What you did**, small and safe first.
4. **How you kept people informed** or got review.
5. **The result and how you know.**

### Weak versus good

**Question: "Tell me about a time you took initiative."**

**Weak (about 25 seconds):**

> "I'm someone who always takes initiative. If I see something that needs to be done, I just do it. For example, I helped out on other tasks when my team was busy, and I always stayed late to make sure things were completed. My manager appreciated that I was proactive."

**Why weak:** no specific event. "Stayed late" is effort, not ownership. Nothing shows a decision.

**Good (about 90 seconds, about 230 words):**

> "Our test suite took twenty-five minutes, and people had stopped running it before merging. Nobody owned it: it wasn't on anyone's list.
>
> I wanted to know why before I did anything, so I spent an afternoon timing it. About sixty percent of the time was one setup step, which created the same database tables in every test file.
>
> I changed it to run once for the whole suite. That touched shared files, so I did not just merge it. I wrote a short description with the before and after numbers, tagged the tech lead, and asked two teammates who knew the test setup to review. One of them found a case where two tests relied on leftover data, and I fixed that.
>
> The suite dropped from twenty-five to nine minutes. Over the next month, the number of broken builds on the main branch fell from about eight to two, because people ran tests before pushing again.
>
> I also put a short note in the team wiki on how to keep the suite fast, so it wouldn't slide back.
>
> The lesson for me is to measure first, fix the biggest piece, and ask for review when I touch things other people depend on."

**Why good:** a clear problem with a cost, evidence before action, a safe process (review), a number result, and a step to make the fix last.

### Doing something without approval

**Question: "Have you ever worked on something without your manager's approval?"** (a real question from a public list.)

**Good (about 80 seconds):**

> "Yes, in a small way. I noticed that our error alerts were firing for a harmless warning about thirty times a day, so people had begun to ignore the alert channel. I was the on-call that week, and I did not have time to ask for a ticket.
>
> I decided I could change one thing without asking: I silenced that one known-harmless warning for seven days, with a note in the alert config explaining why and the date it would come back. That was easy to reverse and affected nothing else.
>
> I told my manager the same day in a short message: what I silenced, why, and when it returns. She said it was fine and asked me to make a proper ticket to fix the cause. I did, and the root cause was fixed the next sprint.
>
> I think acting on small, reversible things is fine, as long as I tell people right away. For anything bigger, such as changing a customer-facing feature or spending money, I'd ask first."

**Why good:** it shows the **boundary** the speaker uses: small, reversible, communicated. A weak answer would say "I just did it and they were fine with it."

## Type 2: Leading a project

### What the interviewer wants

Real questions: "Tell me about a project you led." "Talk about a project you are most passionate about." "Take me through a product you launched from start to end." "Tell me about the most interesting project you have worked on."

You don't need the title "lead" to have led. Leading means that you set direction, made decisions, kept others aligned, and were accountable for the result.

They look for:

1. **Goal clarity.** You knew what success meant.
2. **Planning.** You broke it down, saw risks, ordered the work.
3. **Communication.** Others always knew the status.
4. **People.** You used each person's strengths, and helped those who were stuck.
5. **Results.** The outcome and the cost.

### The shape: goal, plan, people, hurdles, result

1. **The goal and why it mattered**, with the deadline or constraint.
2. **Your role** and the team size.
3. **The plan**: how you split the work, what you put first, how you managed risk.
4. **People and communication**: how you kept everyone aligned.
5. **A hurdle** and how you dealt with it.
6. **The result** with numbers, plus a short reflection.

### Weak versus good

**Question: "Tell me about a project you led."**

**Weak (about 30 seconds):**

> "I led a team that built an e-commerce website. I was the team lead, so I assigned tasks, and we met every day. It was a big project with many parts. We used React for the front end and Node for the back end. In the end we delivered it successfully and the client was happy."

**Why weak:** all technologies, no decisions. "Assigned tasks" is management by list. No numbers, no hurdle, no reflection.

**Good (about 110 seconds, about 280 words):**

> "I led the move of our company's customer notifications from a hand-written script to a proper queue-based service. About two million messages a day went through it, and the old script failed silently a few times a month. I was the technical lead for a team of three developers over ten weeks.
>
> First I wrote a two-page proposal: the problem, the options we had considered, and why I recommended a queue. I sent it to the team and to the support lead, because support felt the failures first. Their comments changed the plan: they asked for a way to see failed messages, so I added that as a first-week task.
>
> I split the work so that the riskiest part, retries and duplicates, came first. I took that part myself. Each of the others owned a clear piece, and we met for fifteen minutes each morning. I sent a short written update every Friday with three lines: done, next, risks.
>
> The biggest hurdle was in week six. We found that a few customers could receive the same message twice after a restart. I stopped the rollout, we added an idempotency check, which means that a repeated message is recognised and ignored, and then restarted with one percent of traffic, then ten, then all.
>
> After launch, failed messages fell from about three percent to under half a percent, and the support team had a page to see what failed and why. We finished one week late because of the duplicates, and I told my manager about the risk in week six, not week ten.
>
> If I did it again I'd write the duplicate test on day one."

**Why good:** it shows structure: proposal, risk-first order, communication, one problem handled well, numbers, honesty about the one-week slip, and a reflection.

### Leading without authority

If you were not the official lead, say so directly. It is not a weakness.

> "I wasn't the team lead, but I volunteered to coordinate the move to the new payment provider because it touched three teams and nobody owned the whole thing. I made a shared checklist of every place we called the old provider, found eleven, and asked each team to pick theirs. I checked the list every two days and chased blockers. The move was done on the planned date with no payment failures."

## Type 3: Mentoring and growing others

### What the interviewer wants

Real question forms: "Tell me about a time you helped a colleague grow." "How do you onboard a new team member?" "Have you mentored anyone?" (In a senior interview, "no" is a worry.)

They look for:

1. **You teach, not only tell.** You help people think, not just copy.
2. **You adapt** to the person.
3. **You are patient** and give room to make small mistakes.
4. **They grew**: there is a result.

### The shape: person, need, approach, change, result

1. **Who** (role and level, no private details) and **what they needed**.
2. **How you found out** what they needed, by asking or watching.
3. **Your approach**: pairing, small tasks, reviews, questions.
4. **What changed in them**, with a sign you can point to.
5. **What you learned about mentoring.**

### Weak versus good

**Question: "Tell me about a time you mentored someone."**

**Weak (about 20 seconds):**

> "I've mentored a few juniors. I answer their questions and review their code, and I'm always available. They all did well and I enjoy helping people."

**Why weak:** no person, no approach, no result. Answering questions is helping, not mentoring.

**Good (about 90 seconds, about 230 words):**

> "Last year a new graduate joined our team, and after a month he was quiet in reviews and slow on tasks. I didn't want to guess, so I asked for a half-hour chat and asked what was hardest. He said he was afraid to ask questions because everyone seemed busy, and he spent hours stuck.
>
> I set up a thirty-minute pairing slot with him twice a week, and I made a rule that was easy for him to follow: if you are stuck for thirty minutes, ask. I also stopped giving him the answer. When he asked how to debug a failing API call, I asked him what he had tried and what he thought the next step was. Mostly he already knew.
>
> I gave him a small task each week with a clear finish, so he could feel progress: a bug fix first, then a small feature, then one he designed himself.
>
> After about three months, he was picking up tickets alone, and his pull requests needed far fewer changes. In his review, his manager said he had become a reliable part of the team, and he started helping a newer intern.
>
> What I learned is that most of mentoring is making it safe to ask, and asking questions back instead of handing out the answer."

**Why good:** a specific person, the discovery of the real problem, a method, a visible result, and a lesson. It does not boast.

### If you have never mentored

Don't invent. Use what you have: helping an intern, onboarding a teammate, running a lunch talk, answering questions in an open-source project, or tutoring. State the scale honestly: "It was informal, over about six weeks."

## Type 4: Deciding with missing information

### What the interviewer wants

Real question forms: "Tell me about a time you had to make a decision without all the facts." "How do you handle ambiguity?" "Tell me about a time requirements were unclear."

They want to see that you do not freeze, and that you do not gamble blindly either.

### The shape: what I knew, what I didn't, how I reduced risk, decision, learning

1. **The decision** and why it could not wait.
2. **What you knew and did not know.** Be explicit.
3. **How you reduced the unknowns** quickly: a small test, asking an expert, a rough number.
4. **How you made it reversible**, or what your fallback was.
5. **The decision, the reasoning and the outcome.**

### Weak versus good

**Question: "Tell me about a time you had to decide without all the information."**

**Weak (about 20 seconds):**

> "I trust my gut. When I don't have all the information, I just go with what feels right, and it usually works out. In one project I picked a database without much research, and it worked fine."

**Why weak:** gut feeling and luck. It sounds risky and unreflective.

**Good (about 100 seconds, about 250 words):**

> "We had to choose, within a week, how to store user uploads for a new feature. We didn't know how many users would upload or how large the files would be, because the feature was new.
>
> I listed what I knew: files up to maybe 20 megabytes, a launch in six weeks, a team with no experience of running file storage. And what I didn't know: the volume. I asked sales for their best and worst guess. They said between five hundred and fifty thousand uploads a month. That is a wide range.
>
> Instead of waiting for certainty, I asked: what is the choice that is safe in both cases? A managed cloud storage service was cheap at low volume and scaled with no work at high volume. Self-hosting would be cheaper at the high end, but risky for a team that had not run it.
>
> I picked the managed service. I also put all file access behind one small module, so that if costs grew, we could swap the storage later by changing one place. I wrote this down in a short decision note with the date and the numbers that would make us revisit it, which was above fifty thousand uploads a month.
>
> In the first six months we had about four thousand uploads a month, so the choice was cheap and we never had to think about it.
>
> What I do now is turn 'I don't know' into a range, pick the option that is safe at both ends, and make the choice easy to undo."

**Why good:** it shows a method: knowns and unknowns, ranges, the choice safe at both ends, reversibility, and a trigger to revisit. See [Team Lead Lesson 0 (how a lead thinks)](/team-lead/lesson-0) and [Lesson 12 (technical strategy)](/team-lead/lesson-12) for the same thinking.

### Unclear requirements

> "Our product manager asked for a 'better search' with no more detail. Instead of guessing, I looked at what people searched for in the last month, and found that about a third of searches returned nothing, mostly because of spelling and word endings. I took that to her with a simple suggestion: fix the empty results first, because it was the clearest failure. She agreed. We handled spelling and plurals in two weeks, and empty searches fell from about a third to about a tenth. I asked for the larger 'better search' work to be planned later with real data."

## Type 5: Measuring your impact

### Why numbers matter

"I improved performance" can mean anything. "I cut the median page load from 4.1 to 1.6 seconds" tells the interviewer your level, your method, and your honesty. The best stories include a **baseline** (before), a **change** (what you did) and a **result** (after).

### What if I have no numbers?

Most people do not keep numbers. You can still find them. Ask these questions about each project:

| Question | Example of a number |
| --- | --- |
| How long did it take before and after? | Build time 25 min to 9 min |
| How often did something fail? | Failed jobs 3 percent to 0.5 percent |
| How many people use it? | 400 support agents |
| How many requests or rows? | 2 million messages a day |
| How much time did it save a person? | 30 minutes a day for 10 people |
| How much money? | About 2,000 per month in hosting |
| How many tickets or incidents? | 14 support requests to 3 |
| How big was the team? | Three developers, ten weeks |
| How big was the risk? | Payments for all customers |

### Honest estimates

If you really can't recall an exact figure, **estimate openly**:

- "I don't have the exact number, but I believe it was around forty percent."
- "It saved roughly an hour a week for each of eight people, so about eight hours a week."
- "I'd estimate; I can show you how I calculated it."

This is better than inventing a precise number such as 37.4 percent. Interviewers can sense false precision. Also, never claim impact that you did not cause. If a launch coincided with a marketing campaign, say so.

### A weak and a good impact answer

**Question: "What is your biggest achievement in your previous projects?"** (a real question from a public list)

**Weak (about 20 seconds):**

> "My biggest achievement was optimising the application. I improved the performance a lot and the users were happy. It was a very difficult task and I worked hard on it."

**Why weak:** nothing measurable. "A lot" and "happy" are empty.

**Good (about 90 seconds):**

> "My biggest achievement was making our order history page usable for large customers. It took about twelve seconds to load for accounts with many orders, and our top fifteen customers, who bring in a large share of revenue, were complaining.
>
> I found that the page ran one query per order to get its items, so a customer with five hundred orders caused five hundred queries. I changed it to fetch everything with two queries, and added an index on the column we filtered by. I also tested with a copy of the biggest customer's data, so I knew it would hold up.
>
> The load time fell from about twelve seconds to under one. Support tickets about slow orders went from around thirty a month to about five. One of the large customers mentioned the change in a renewal call, which our account manager told me about.
>
> I'm proud of it because it was not a clever technique. It was finding the real cause by measuring, and checking the fix on realistic data."

**Why good:** baseline, cause, change, result, and proof from a third party (the account manager). The tone is modest.

### Impact beyond code

Seniors also have impact that doesn't show as lines of code:

- **Time saved for others**: a tool or a doc that 20 people use.
- **Risk removed**: a migration that avoided an outage.
- **People grown**: someone you mentored who was promoted.
- **Decisions improved**: a proposal that stopped the team building the wrong thing.
- **Culture**: starting a habit such as short design notes.

A strong closing line for a senior story: "The result was the number, but the lasting result is that the team now does X by default."

## Putting several skills in one story

The best stories have more than one signal. The notification-queue story above shows leadership, handling a problem under pressure, communication and impact. That is why a good story bank has five or six stories and not fifty. When a question arrives, you choose the story and **steer** it to the signal. For ownership, you start with "nobody owned it". For leadership you start with "I was the lead for a team of three". For impact you start with the baseline number. The same facts, a different first sentence.

## Questions about pride and passion

Some questions ask what excites you. "What are you excited about?" "Talk about a project where you did your best work." "What does your best day of work look like?" These are not STAR questions; they ask for energy and honesty. A short model (about 40 seconds):

> "My best days are when I'm helping make something hard feel simple. Last month I spent a day with a support agent watching her use our admin page. I saw her click eleven times to do something that should take two. I fixed it that week, and she told me it saved her about an hour a day. I like days where I can see a real person's work get easier. I'm less excited by work that nobody will ever notice."

## Try it

1. Pick your three best projects. For each, fill the impact numbers section. If you have none, estimate with the table above and note "estimate."
2. Take one task you did that "wasn't your job". Write the ownership story using notice, decide, act, tell, check.
3. Rewrite this sentence so that it shows scope: "I helped with the migration."
4. Write a mentoring story about a real person. If you have never mentored, write one about onboarding or explaining something to a new person.
5. Write the sentence you'd use to open your leadership story if the question were about ownership, then again if it were about impact. Notice how only the first sentence changes.

### Answers

1. A project with no number is usually missing one of: time, count of users, count of failures, or money. Ask how it was before.
2. A good ownership story includes a small first step and a message to someone, not a secret solo project.
3. A rewrite with scope: "I owned the database part of the migration: I planned the order, wrote the checks, and ran the cutover for two of the five services." It names your piece.
4. A mentoring story needs one specific person and one visible change, not "I help everyone."
5. For ownership: "Nobody owned the notifications, and they were failing quietly." For impact: "Failed messages were three percent of two million a day." Same story, different first sentence.

## Interview questions

### Q1. Tell me about a time you took ownership of something that wasn't your job. (Must know)

Our test suite took 25 minutes and people had stopped running it. Nobody owned it. I timed it and found that sixty percent of the time was one repeated setup step. I changed it to run once. Because it touched shared files, I wrote the before and after numbers and asked two teammates to review. The suite dropped to nine minutes and broken builds on the main branch fell from about eight a month to two. I also added a short note on how to keep it fast.

### Q2. Tell me about a project you led. (Must know)

I led the move of our customer notifications to a queue-based service, with three developers over ten weeks. I wrote a two-page proposal and shared it with support, who asked for a failed-message page, which I added first. I did the riskiest part myself, retries and duplicates, and sent a Friday update with done, next and risks. In week six we found duplicates after restarts, so I paused the rollout and added a duplicate check. Failures fell from about three percent to under half a percent. We finished a week late, and I'd write the duplicate test on day one next time.

### Q3. What is something you had to push for in a previous project? (Senior signal)

I pushed for a staged rollout of a new billing page. The team wanted to ship to everyone on release day. I explained that a bug in billing would affect money, and I suggested one percent, then ten, then all, over three days, with a simple dashboard. I knew it added work, so I built the dashboard myself. On day one, at one percent, we saw a rounding error in one currency, and fixed it before most customers saw it. I learned that I get more support for a safety measure when I do part of the work.

### Q4. Have you ever worked on something without your manager's approval? (Trap)

The trap is to boast about ignoring rules. My honest answer is: only small, reversible things, and I tell my manager at once. For example, as on-call I silenced one known harmless alert for seven days, with a note and a return date, and I messaged my manager the same day. She approved and asked for a ticket for the root cause. For anything bigger, like changing what customers see, I ask first.

### Q5. Tell me about a time you mentored someone. (Must know)

A new graduate on my team was quiet and stuck for hours. I asked him what was hardest, and he said he was afraid to bother busy people. I set up two pairing sessions a week and a simple rule: stuck for thirty minutes, then ask. I answered his questions with questions, like what he had tried. I gave him small tasks of growing size. After three months he took tickets alone and began helping an intern. My main lesson is to make it safe to ask.

### Q6. Tell me about a time you had to make a decision with incomplete information. (Must know)

We had to choose storage for uploads in a week, with no idea of the volume. I asked sales for a best and worst guess, which was five hundred to fifty thousand uploads a month. I chose managed storage, because it was safe at both ends, and put all file access behind one module so we could swap later. I wrote down that we would revisit above fifty thousand uploads. Real use was about four thousand a month, so it was cheap and stayed simple.

### Q7. How do you handle ambiguity? (Must know)

I turn the unknowns into a short list and reduce the biggest ones first. If the requirement is vague, I look for data, such as what users search for, and bring a small concrete proposal to the person who asked. For example, with a request for "better search", I found that a third of searches returned nothing. We fixed that first and empty results fell to about a tenth. I prefer to make a small reversible choice and learn, rather than wait for full clarity.

### Q8. What is your biggest achievement? (Must know)

Making order history usable for large customers. It took twelve seconds for accounts with many orders, and top customers complained. I found it ran one query per order, changed it to two queries plus an index, and tested on a copy of the biggest customer's data. It dropped to under a second, and tickets about slow orders went from about thirty a month to five. I'm proud of it because I found the cause by measuring, not guessing.

### Q9. How do you measure the impact of your work? (Senior signal)

I try to write the baseline before I start, such as load time, failure rate or hours spent. After the change, I measure again. When I can't measure directly, I use a proxy, like support tickets for confusion. If I can't get an exact figure, I say it is an estimate and explain how I got it. I also try to include effects on people, such as time saved for the support team, because those are part of the real value.

### Q10. Tell me about a time you led without authority. (Senior signal)

A move to a new payment provider touched three teams and nobody owned it. I volunteered to coordinate. I made one shared checklist of every place the old provider was called, found eleven, and asked each team to take theirs. I reviewed the list every two days and chased blockers. We finished on the planned date with no payment failures. I learned that being the person who keeps the list visible is often enough to lead.

### Q11. Tell me about a time you had to meet a tight deadline. (Must know)

I had six weeks for a checkout rebuild that needed ten. I listed all features as must, nice or later, and asked the product manager to challenge it. We dropped saved cards and a coupon screen, and I wrote that down. I built the risky payment step first, with a daily review. Each Friday I sent a three-line update, and in week four I flagged the address form as at risk so the designer could simplify it. We shipped four days early, and saved cards followed a month later.

### Q12. What does your best day of work look like? (Must know)

A day when I make something hard feel simple for a real person. Last month I watched a support agent click eleven times to finish a task that should take two. I fixed it that week and she said it saved her about an hour a day. I like days when I can point to a real person whose work got easier.

## Key takeaways

- These stories show your **level**: scope of what you owned, the decisions you made, and the people you brought along.
- **Ownership:** notice, measure, act small and safe, tell people, check the result.
- **Leading:** a clear goal, a risk-first plan, steady communication, one hurdle handled, a result, and a reflection.
- **Mentoring:** a specific person, a method that builds their skill, a visible change.
- **Decisions with missing data:** list knowns and unknowns, use ranges, choose what's safe at both ends, make it reversible, set a trigger to revisit.
- **Impact:** baseline, change, result. Estimate openly when you lack data, and don't claim credit for what you didn't cause.
- You do not need a title to tell a leadership story, only real decisions.

## Words used in this lesson

- **Mock interview** is a practice interview, with a friend, a mentor, a paid service or an AI tool, run like the real one.
- **Scorecard** is a sheet with the things being scored and a number for each.
- **Focus area** is one thing interviewers look for, such as proactivity or growth.
- **Story bank** is your short list of 4 to 6 real work stories you can reshape to answer many questions.
- **STAR** means Situation, Task, Action, Result. **STAR(R)** adds Reflection, what you learned.
- **Probe** (or follow-up) is the extra question an interviewer asks to dig into a detail.
- **Filler words** are "um", "like", "you know" and similar, used to fill silence.
- **Debrief** is the talk after the interview, where you or the interviewers review what happened.
- **Scope** is the size of the problem or group you worked with (one task, a team, many teams).

## The story bank first

Before any mock, build your bank. The handbook's advice is: do not try to memorise 50 answers. Pick **3 to 5 projects** with high impact, complexity and your own involvement, and write each as a STAR(R) story. Most questions can be answered by reshaping one of these.

Fill in this table once. I recommend a notebook or a document.

| Story name (3 words) | Situation (1 line) | Your action (the repeatable behaviors) | Result (a number if possible) | Lesson learned | Good for questions about... |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Export rewrite | Customers got empty CSV files | Found the bug, wrote tests, led the fix across 2 services | Complaints fell from 12 a week to 0 | Check helpers used in many places | Perseverance, proactivity, impact |
| Release quarrel | Disagreed with a teammate about a release plan | Listened, proposed a test, agreed to a smaller step | Release went on time, no incident | Ask for the reason before the solution | Conflict, empathy |
| Night outage | Our checkout failed after my change | Rolled back, told the manager, wrote the post-incident note | Fixed in 25 minutes; new test added | Tell early, fix first | Failure, growth |
| New team tool | Slow, manual onboarding | Wrote docs and a setup script without being asked | New hire setup from 2 days to 3 hours | Small tools save a lot of time | Initiative, unstructured work |
| Mentoring Sam | A junior was stuck on reviews | Weekly 30-minute sessions, shared checklists | Sam shipped alone after 2 months | Teach the "why" | Leadership, empathy |

(The stories above are made up. Use real ones.)

Then check the **gaps**: do you have a story for failure, conflict, leadership, ambiguity, pressure, learning something new, and a decision with missing information? If any is empty, think about your past year again. A small story is better than none.

For each story, write the numbers you remember (users, minutes, money, tickets, percent). Numbers make a story believable. If you are not sure of a number, say "about" and give a range. Never invent one.

## What the interviewer scores

The handbook's rubric page lists eight focus areas. Here they are with the question that usually checks each and what the interviewer wants in plain words:

| Focus area | A question that checks it | What they want to hear |
|---|---|---|
| Motivation | "What project are you most proud of?" | What drives you; real excitement and clear reasons |
| Proactivity | "Tell me about a time you changed something outside your normal scope." | You noticed a problem and acted without being told |
| Unstructured environment | "Tell me about an unclear task." | You made it clear: asked, wrote down assumptions, took ownership |
| Perseverance | "Tell me about a project that took longer than expected." | You kept going, solved blockers one by one |
| Conflict resolution | "Tell me about a disagreement with a coworker." | You worked it through, not around or over the person |
| Empathy | (Often in the same story) | You understood the other view and said it |
| Growth | "Tell me about a mistake and what you learned." | Honest ownership and a real change afterward |
| Communication | (Every answer) | Clear, organised, the right length, easy to follow |

The page also says interviewers look at the **scope** of each story. Roughly: a junior story is about a task or one coworker, a senior story is about a project that affected the whole team and needed several people, a staff story is about work across teams. So use your biggest honest story for each question, and say who else was involved and how big the effect was.

## The scorecard

Use this for a mock, or to review a recording of yourself. Score each row from 1 to 4 right after each answer, then total at the end. Write a short piece of **evidence** for each score. A number without evidence is a mood.

| Score | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 1 | No signal. The answer missed the question or hid the real story. |
| 2 | Weak. A story is there but the action is unclear, "we" instead of "I", or there is no result or lesson. |
| 3 | Solid. Clear story, your own actions, a result, reasonable scope. |
| 4 | Strong. All of 3, plus numbers, a thoughtful reflection, and scope above what is expected for the level. |


My score bands (rules of thumb, not from the handbook):

- **28 to 32:** strong. Polish and keep practising.
- **24 to 27:** good. Fix the lowest row.
- **20 to 23:** borderline. Work on the story bank and structure.
- **Below 20:** not ready. Do the plan below, then mock again.
- **Any row of 1** is a red alert on its own, whatever the total.

## A full mock round

Here is a mock round with a fictional candidate. The candidate is a developer with four years of experience applying for a mid-level backend role. The interviewer is fictional. I show the question, the answer as it could be spoken, then the score with evidence. Read each answer **out loud**. Spoken answers sound different from written ones.

### Question 1: Tell me about yourself. (Motivation, Communication)

> **Candidate:** "Sure. I'm a backend developer with four years of experience, mostly building APIs and background jobs for a delivery-tracking product. In my current role I own the invoicing and export services. Last year I led a fix of our export feature that took customer complaints from about twelve a week to zero, and I enjoyed it because it combined debugging, talking to support and teaching the team. Before that I worked on a smaller team where I learned to ship fast and to run what I ship. Now I'm looking for a team where I can work on reliability and payments at a bigger scale, and your checkout work is exactly that. I'm excited to talk about it."

**Score:** Motivation 3, Communication 3. Evidence: about 60 seconds; present, past, future structure; one number (twelve to zero); a clear link to the company. To get a 4: add one more sentence on why checkout, with a specific thing learned from the company's public engineering posts.

### Question 2: What project are you most proud of? (Motivation, Perseverance)

> **Candidate:** "The export rewrite. Situation: customers with a lot of data were getting empty CSV files, and support got about twelve complaints a week. My task was to find the cause and fix it. I started by reproducing it with a test of 5,000 rows, and I found a paging bug in a helper that was also used in three other places. I fixed the helper, wrote tests, and opened a ticket for the other three places, which I then fixed over the next week with a teammate. The result was that complaints dropped to zero within a month and the export time fell from about 90 seconds to 20. What I'm proud of is not the fix itself but that I checked the other places, because the same bug was waiting there. What I would do differently: add the test earlier. I now write a test for the real-size case first."

**Score:** Motivation 3, Perseverance 3, Growth 3. Evidence: STAR(R) shape; numbers (twelve to zero, 90 to 20 seconds); a reflection. To get a 4: say how many people the work affected and mention the cross-team teammate, to show scope above one person.

### Question 3: Tell me about an unclear task. (Unstructured work, Proactivity)

> **Candidate:** "My manager asked me to 'make onboarding faster' for new developers. That was all. I first asked what 'faster' meant, and we agreed that the number to improve was the time from the first day to the first merged pull request. It was about six days. I interviewed the last three hires, and they all said the setup instructions were out of date and the local database was hard to start. I wrote down my assumptions and the plan in a short document and sent it to my manager and two teammates, and asked for objections by Friday. Nobody objected, so I wrote a setup script and fixed the docs. The next hire got to her first merge in two days. I also asked her to correct the docs as she went. What I learned: when a task is vague, I first turn it into a number and write down my guesses so people can correct me early."

**Score:** Unstructured 4, Proactivity 3. Evidence: asked for a measure, collected facts, wrote assumptions, set a deadline for objections, a result (six days to two). Scope: team level.

### Question 4: Tell me about a conflict with a coworker. (Conflict resolution, Empathy)

First, a **weak** answer, as many people give it:

> **Candidate (weak):** "I had a coworker who always disagreed with me in meetings. He was very stubborn and did not listen. In the end I just did it my way and the project went fine. I try to avoid conflict if I can."

**Score:** Conflict 1 or 2, Empathy 1. Evidence: blame ("stubborn"), no specific story, no action that resolved it, the result was "I won", and the lesson is "avoid". An interviewer learns that this person may not work well with others.

Now a **strong** answer to the same question (a different, honest story):

> **Candidate (strong):** "A few months ago, a teammate named Sam and I disagreed about how to release a change to the invoice calculation. Situation: I wanted to release the whole change at once on Friday, because the customer wanted it. Sam wanted to release it behind a feature flag in two steps, because he was worried about wrong totals. The disagreement got tense in chat. My task was to get us to a plan we both trusted without missing the customer date. First, I moved from chat to a ten-minute call. I asked Sam what exactly he was worried about. He said the old and new calculation could differ in rare cases, like invoices with several taxes, and he had seen a similar bug before. That was a good point and I had not considered it. I suggested a small test: run both calculations on last month's invoices and compare. We did that together the same afternoon, and found four invoices where the totals were different. We fixed the cause, released behind a flag on Friday, and turned it on for all customers on Monday. The customer date was met on the flag release, and we had no wrong invoices. What I learned is to ask for the specific worry before arguing the solution, and that a small test can end a debate faster than opinions. Sam and I now review each other's money-related changes."

**Score:** Conflict 4, Empathy 3. Evidence: named a clear conflict, asked to understand, used data, found a result that satisfied both, a lasting relationship change. To keep a 4, make sure the story did not make the other person look foolish; here Sam's point was valid and said so.

Why the weak answer failed: it had no specific event, it used a label ("stubborn") instead of a behavior, the candidate did nothing to understand the other person, and the lesson was avoidance. The strong answer fixed each of these.

### Question 5: Tell me about a mistake you made. (Growth)

> **Candidate:** "In March I released a change to the tax calculation without running the full invoice test set, because I was in a hurry. About 20 minutes after the release, support told me that some invoices had wrong totals. I immediately rolled back the change, told my manager in a message with the facts, and found that 40 invoices were affected. I wrote a script to fix them, and informed support so they could tell the customers. The same day I wrote a short post-incident note and added a test that covers multi-tax invoices. What I changed in how I work: I no longer skip the full test set, even for small changes, and I added it to the release checklist. In the following six months I had no similar incident. I'm not proud of the mistake, but I'm proud of how quickly we recovered."

**Score:** Growth 4. Evidence: a real mistake, ownership, speed to tell and to fix, concrete changes in process, results over time. It did not blame anyone. (Do not pick a fake mistake like "I work too hard". Interviewers see through it.)

### Question 6: Tell me about a project that took longer than expected. (Perseverance)

> **Candidate:** "The export rewrite is a good example, because I first planned two days and it took two weeks. After I fixed the first bug, a second one appeared in the date handling for other time zones. I kept a list of the blockers and solved them one by one. I told my manager in the first week that it would slip, with a new estimate range of two weeks, and what we could cut if the date was fixed. We shipped the main fix on day four and the rest on day ten. Looking back, my first estimate did not include unknowns. Now I give estimates as ranges and name what I do not know yet."

**Score:** Perseverance 3. Evidence: kept going, told early, learned about estimates. To get a 4: a larger scope (blockers affecting several people) and a number for impact.

### Question 7: Do you have any questions for us? (Motivation, Communication)

> **Candidate:** "Yes, two. You mentioned the team is working to make checkout more reliable. What is the hardest part of that right now? And what does success look like for this role at six months?"

**Score:** Motivation 3 (adds to the earlier). Evidence: tied to what the interviewer said; open questions; short. More examples in [lesson 5](/behavioral-interview/lesson-5).

| Proactivity | 3 | Q3: onboarding project started from a vague ask; checked other places in Q2 |

### Probes (follow-up questions) to practise

Interviewers love to dig. Practise answers to these on each story:

- "What was your exact role? What did others do?"
- "What would you do differently?"
- "What was the hardest decision, and what else did you consider?"
- "How did you measure success? How do you know it was the result of your work?"
- "What did your manager (or the other person) say about it afterwards?"
- "Tell me more about that." (Give another detail, not the same sentence again.)

A sample probe-and-answer:

> **Interviewer:** You said you wrote a script to fix 40 invoices. How did you check the script was safe?
>
> **Candidate:** I first ran it on a copy of the data and compared the totals by hand for five invoices. Then I ran it on all 40 with a "dry run" mode that only prints the changes. My manager reviewed the output, and only then did I run the real update, in a transaction, with a backup first.

## Spoken delivery: the part people forget

Written text is not speech. Some practice rules:

1. **Length:** most answers should be 1.5 to 3 minutes. Under one minute is too thin, over four loses the listener. Time yourself.
2. **Pause before you answer.** Two or three seconds to think looks calm. Say "Let me think of a good example" if you need it.
3. **Start with the point.** "A time I disagreed with a teammate was last March." Then the story.
4. **Say "I" for what you did.** Say "we" for what the team did. Many candidates say "we" for everything, and the interviewer cannot tell what they did.
5. **Slow down at the result.** The result is what people remember. Say the number clearly.
6. **Avoid reading notes.** Use bullet points of 3 to 5 words, not sentences.
7. **Keep filler words small.** Record yourself once and count them. A few are normal.
8. **On video:** look at the camera at the key moments, have light on your face, and test the sound.
9. **Do not blame.** Describe behaviors and facts. Never name a person in a bad light.
10. **Be honest.** If you do not have a story, say so and offer the closest one: "I haven't led a team, but I did lead a small project, and here is what I did."

> [!warn] **The biggest traps:** making up a story (you cannot survive probes), saying "we" for everything, long setup with no result, blaming, a fake weakness, and answering a different question than the one asked. Listen to the question, and if you are not sure, ask: "Do you mean a technical disagreement or a personal one?"

## How to run a mock

### With a friend or a mentor

1. Give them your scorecard and the list of questions (use the common questions in the handbook's list, or the ones in lessons 1 to 4).
2. They ask 6 or 7 questions, with probes, in a time of 45 to 60 minutes.
3. They write evidence under each row. They do not give hints.
4. Afterwards you talk for 15 minutes: first what **you** think went well, then their notes.
5. Write your top two fixes. Next mock, check those two first.

For tips on how to run mocks (roles, time limits, notes), career lesson 2 has a longer routine you can reuse for behavioral rounds.

### Alone

1. Record audio or video on your phone.
2. Ask yourself a question from a random list (write 20 on slips of paper and draw one).
3. Answer out loud for up to 3 minutes without notes.
4. Play it back once. Score yourself with the scorecard. Note filler words, length and missing numbers.
5. Re-record the weakest answer. Compare.

### With an AI tool

The handbook says it is natural to use a chatbot to ask you questions and give feedback, and to encourage it to ask follow-ups. If you do, a prompt like this helps (it is mine):

```text
Act as an interviewer for a mid-level backend developer role. Ask me one
behavioral question at a time. After my answer, ask one follow-up probe.
Then score my answer from 1 to 4 on: clarity, my own actions (I vs we),
result with a number, and reflection. Say what is missing. Do not give me
a model answer unless I ask.
```

An AI tool does not replace a human. It cannot see your face, hear your tone or judge scope like a real manager would. And never paste private company data or secrets into it.

## The one-week preparation plan

Assume your interview is in 7 days and you have about one hour a day (two on the weekend). Adjust the days. If you have more time, stretch it. If you have less, do days 1, 3, 5 and 7.

| Day | Time | Task | Output |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Day 1** | 60 min | Research the company: product, values, recent news, the job post. List 3 values or needs from the post. Build your story bank table (5 stories). | Table filled. 3 values written. |
| **Day 2** | 60 min | Write the "big three": tell me about yourself, why this company, why leaving. Write bullet points, not sentences. (See [lesson 1](/behavioral-interview/lesson-1).) Say each out loud 3 times. | Three 90-second answers in bullets. |
| **Day 3** | 60 min | Write STAR(R) for your conflict, failure and ambiguity stories. Add one number to each. Practise probes. (See [lessons 2](/behavioral-interview/lesson-2) and [4](/behavioral-interview/lesson-4).) | Three stories, numbers added. |
| **Day 4** | 60 min | Write STAR(R) for your leadership and impact stories (see [lesson 3](/behavioral-interview/lesson-3)). Prepare your honest answers on weakness, gaps or short jobs if they apply. | Two more stories. Weakness answer. |
| **Day 5** | 60 min | Prepare 8 questions to ask (see [lesson 5](/behavioral-interview/lesson-5)). Prepare your salary plan: three numbers, the script for "expectations" (see [lesson 6](/behavioral-interview/lesson-6)). | 8 questions. 3 numbers. |
| **Day 6** | 90 min | Full mock with a friend, or alone with a recording. Score with the scorecard. Pick two fixes. | Scorecard filled. Two fixes. |
| **Day 7** | 45 min (the day before) | Re-do the two fixes. Re-read your bullets once. Prepare the logistics (below). Sleep early. Do **not** cram new stories. | Checklist done. Rest. |

If the interview is in a month, do the same plan but spend a week on each step, and do three mocks, with at least one with a person who does not know you well.

## Checklist for the day

**The night before**

```text
[ ] Interview time, place or link, and time zone confirmed (and written in the calendar)
[ ] Name and role of each interviewer noted
[ ] Resume and job post printed or open
[ ] Story bank bullets and your 8 questions in a notebook
[ ] Clothes chosen; for video, background and light tested
[ ] Laptop charged; charger, headphones, backup phone data
[ ] Phone on silent for tomorrow
[ ] Route or travel time checked (arrive 10 to 15 minutes early)
[ ] Sleep 7 or more hours (this matters more than one more hour of study)
```

**One hour before**

```text
[ ] Light meal; water
[ ] Read the one-page summary: 3 values, 5 story names, 3 numbers
[ ] Say the "tell me about yourself" answer once out loud
[ ] For video: open the link 5 minutes early, test camera and sound
[ ] Breathe slowly: in for 4 seconds, out for 6, five times
```

**During the interview**

```text
[ ] Smile and greet by name
[ ] Pause two seconds before answering
[ ] Start each answer with the point
[ ] "I" for what I did, "we" for what the team did
[ ] A number in the result
[ ] Check: "Does that answer your question? I can go deeper on X."
[ ] Use my notebook for questions
[ ] Ask 3 to 5 questions at the end; ask about next steps
```

**After the interview (same day)**

```text
[ ] Send a thank-you email (see lesson 5)
[ ] Write down every question I was asked and how I felt about my answer
[ ] Score myself with the scorecard
[ ] Add any new stories or fixes to my bank
[ ] If salary comes up, stay calm and use lesson 6
[ ] If I get rejected, ask for feedback (script in lesson 7) and treat it as data
```

## When it goes badly

You will, sometimes, give a bad answer or have a rough round. It happens to everyone. What matters:

1. **During the interview:** if you realise you gave a weak answer, you can say, "Can I try that again? I think I can explain it better." Most interviewers allow it.
2. **If you freeze:** say "Give me a moment to think." Drink water. Start with the situation.
3. **After:** write down what happened and what to fix. Don't spiral. One round is one data point.
4. **After a rejection:** thank them, ask for feedback once (script in [lesson 7](/behavioral-interview/lesson-7)), and move on. A rejection is rarely about one answer.

## Try it

**Exercise.** Take the question "Tell me about a time you had to learn something new quickly." Do this in 25 minutes:

1. Pick a story from your bank (or a real one from the past year).
2. Write 5 bullet points: Situation, Task, Action (3 things you did), Result (with a number), Reflection.
3. Say the answer out loud and time it. Aim for 2 minutes.
4. Score it with the scorecard rows Growth, Proactivity and Communication, with evidence.
5. Write one probe the interviewer might ask, and your answer.

**Sample answer (made up).**

- Situation: our team had to move from a manual deploy to an automated pipeline, and I had never built one.
- Task: set up a pipeline for the invoicing service in three weeks.
- Action: (1) I read the tool's official guide and built a tiny pipeline for a test service first. (2) I asked a colleague from another team to review my plan for 20 minutes. (3) I wrote a runbook as I went, so others could follow.
- Result: deploy time fell from about 40 minutes to 8, and two other teams copied the setup.
- Reflection: starting with a tiny test service made mistakes cheap; now I do that with any new tool.
- Spoken time: about 1 minute 50 seconds.
- Score: Growth 3 (learning plan clear), Proactivity 3 (asked for review, wrote runbook), Communication 3. To reach 4: say how many people used the runbook and what changed in their work.
- Probe: "What was the hardest part?" Answer: "Handling secrets safely. I learned how the tool stores them and asked the security person to check my setup."

## Interview questions

This final lesson has more questions, covering the whole series.

### Q1. How do you prepare for a behavioral interview? (Must know)

"I build a story bank of four to six real projects, each in STAR with a reflection, and I add a number to the result. I prepare the big three: tell me about yourself, why this company and why I'm leaving. I research the company's values and match stories to them. Then I practise out loud and score myself on a scorecard. I do one or more mocks with another person. I don't memorise answers word for word. I memorise bullet points."

### Q2. What is the STAR method and what are its limits? (Must know)

"STAR is Situation, Task, Action, Result. It keeps a story organised. Its limit is that people spend too long on the situation and too little on what they did. I add a final R for reflection: what I learned and changed. I keep the situation to two or three sentences, and spend most of the time on my actions, because actions are the behaviors the interviewer is trying to predict."

### Q3. Tell me about yourself. (Must know)

"I use present, past, future. I'm a backend developer with four years of experience, now owning the invoicing and export services. Before that I worked on a smaller team where I learned to ship and run what I ship. I'm looking for a team that works on reliability and payments at a bigger scale, and this role is that. I keep it to about a minute, with one number." (More forms in [lesson 1](/behavioral-interview/lesson-1).)

### Q4. What is your greatest weakness? (Trap)

"I pick a real, small weakness, say what I do about it, and show progress. For example: I used to say yes to every request and then miss my own deadlines. Now I show the trade-off to my manager and ask which task should move. I still catch myself wanting to say yes, but I have a routine for it. I don't say 'I work too hard', because that is not a weakness, and I don't pick one that is central to the job." (See [lesson 4](/behavioral-interview/lesson-4).)

### Q5. Tell me about a conflict. How do you handle it when the other person is wrong? (Trap)

"First I check if they are really wrong. I ask what exactly they are worried about. Often they know something I don't. If they are wrong, I use facts or a small test and not a debate. I keep my words about the issue and not about the person. If we still disagree, I explain my view, accept that the owner of the decision decides, and commit. The trap is a story where I win and the other person looks foolish."

### Q6. What do you do when you don't know the answer? (Must know)

"I say so, then I show how I would find out. In a behavioral round, if I have no story for a question, I say 'I haven't faced exactly that, the closest thing I have is...' and tell it honestly. I don't invent a story, because probes will expose it."

### Q7. How do you answer questions about failure without sounding weak? (Senior signal)

"By owning the mistake in one sentence, and spending most of the time on what I did to fix it and what changed. I choose a real mistake of moderate size, not a disaster and not a fake. I give the facts: what happened, how fast I told people, how I fixed it, and what I changed so it won't repeat. Interviewers do not mind failure. They mind blame and no learning."

### Q8. How do you show seniority in a behavioral answer? (Senior signal)

"By scope, judgment and reflection. I choose stories where several people or teams were affected, I explain the trade-offs I considered and why I chose, I mention how I brought others along, and I say what I would do differently. In the handbook's rubric the difference between levels is mostly the scope: the same behavior, but affecting a task, a team or several teams."

### Q9. Why should we hire you? (Must know)

"Three reasons. One: my experience matches the main need in this role, such as building and running reliable APIs, and I can show it with the export project. Two: I work well with others and I communicate early, which I showed in the release disagreement. Three: I'm excited about your checkout work and I'd learn fast. I'd be happy to say more about any of these." I keep it to 30 to 45 seconds.

### Q10. Where do you see yourself in five years? (Trap)

"I want to grow as an engineer who owns bigger systems and helps others grow. I can't say the exact title, and I'm happy to follow where I'm most useful. For the next two years I'd like to deepen my skills in reliability and design, and maybe lead a small project. I'd like to do that here, if the team grows in that direction." I avoid 'your job' or 'running my own company', and I avoid 'I have no idea'.

### Q11. How do you handle nerves in an interview? (Senior signal)

"I prepare, so there are fewer surprises. I do a mock out loud. On the day I sleep, I arrive early, I breathe slowly, and I pause before answering. If I freeze, I say 'give me a moment' and start with the situation. I treat the round as a conversation. I make no medical claims, this is just what works for me, and if nerves are very strong I'd talk to a professional."

### Q12. What do you do after an interview? (Must know)

"I send a short thank-you email the same day that names one real thing from the talk. I write down every question and how I answered, and I score myself. I add what I learned to my story bank. If I'm rejected, I ask politely for feedback once and thank them for whatever I get."

### Q13. How would you decide between two offers? (Senior signal)

"I convert each into a yearly total: base, expected bonus, equity per year, retirement match and benefits, and I compare the cash and the total separately. I look at risks like the cliff and share price. Then I use my team scorecard for manager, learning, hours and stability. In my made-up example from lesson 6, a base of 100,000 gave a higher total than a base of 115,000, but less cash, so the choice depended on my needs." (All numbers there are examples, not market data; check current local data.)

### Q14. What would you do in your first 90 days? (Senior signal)

"In week one I'd learn what success looks like at 30, 60 and 90 days, and write it down with my manager. In the first month I'd ship a small change to learn the process and fix any docs that confuse me. In month two I'd own one small area and ask for feedback. By month three I'd lead a small project and share what I learned. I'd ask my questions in public channels so the answers help the next person." (See [lesson 7](/behavioral-interview/lesson-7).)

### Q15. Do you have any questions for us? (Must know)

"Yes. What is the hardest part of the work the team is doing right now? What does success look like in this role at six months? And what happened the last time something broke in production?" (See [lesson 5](/behavioral-interview/lesson-5).)

### Q16. What is the single most useful habit for behavioral interviews? (Senior signal)

"Practising out loud with feedback. Reading answers and writing them down helps, but speaking uses a different skill. A mock with a scorecard shows the gaps that my own head cannot see, like blame, too much 'we', or no result."

## Key takeaways

- Build a small story bank (4 to 6 real stories) with numbers. Reshape stories for each question. Don't memorise scripts.
- Interviewers score eight areas: motivation, proactivity, unstructured work, perseverance, conflict resolution, empathy, growth and communication. Use them as your scorecard rows, with evidence.
- Spoken answers: 1.5 to 3 minutes, start with the point, "I" for actions, a number in the result, a reflection at the end.
- One weak answer (blame, no action, no lesson) can drop a total from the 'good' band to 'borderline'. Fix the weakest row first.
- Mocks give the best feedback. Record yourself, or use a friend with the scorecard. Use AI tools only as an extra.
- Follow the one-week plan, use the checklist, and treat a bad round as data. Thank-you email, self-score and feedback request afterwards.
- All names, companies and numbers in this series' examples are made up. Check current local data for salary.

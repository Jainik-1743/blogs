> **In one line:** in an incident, the order of operations is **stop the bleeding first, find the cause second, learn third**. Seniors are judged on calm triage, clear communication and a blameless follow-up, not on how fast they guess the root cause.

Senior interviews love incident scenarios because they test judgement under pressure: what do you do in the first five minutes, how do you decide to roll back, and what changes afterwards? This lesson gives you the playbook, then eleven realistic scenarios in the **Symptom → Diagnose → Fix → Prevent** shape.

## The incident playbook

### 1. Detect and declare

- Someone notices (alert, customer report, a dashboard). The first decision is to **declare an incident** early; it is cheap to downgrade and expensive to delay.
- **Assign roles** (even in a small team, say them out loud): an **incident commander** (coordinates, decides), an **investigator** (hands on keyboard), and a **communicator** (updates stakeholders). One person must not be doing all three.
- Open a single channel or "war room" and a running **timeline** (what we saw, what we tried, when). The timeline becomes the postmortem.

### 2. Assess impact and severity

Ask: **Who is affected? How badly? Is it getting worse?** Classify so everyone calibrates (names vary by company):

| Severity | Example | Response |
|---|---|---|
| **SEV1** | Checkout down for everyone, data loss, security breach | Everyone needed now, executive updates every 15 to 30 minutes |
| **SEV2** | Major feature degraded for many users | Dedicated responders, hourly updates |
| **SEV3** | Minor feature or a small group affected | Fix in business hours |

### 3. Mitigate before you diagnose

The goal is to **restore service**, not to understand. In order of speed and safety:

1. **Roll back** the last deploy (or flip a **feature flag** off). If something changed shortly before the symptoms, this is the highest-probability fix.
2. **Scale or shed load** (add instances, enable rate limiting, disable expensive features, serve cached or static fallbacks).
3. **Fail over** (to a replica, another region) if infrastructure is the cause.
4. **Block or isolate** (an abusive client, a bad tenant, a poisoned queue message).
5. Only then, if none of that helps, debug forward and hot-fix.

> [!ok] "Rollback first" is a senior reflex. A rollback you can verify in two minutes beats a clever fix you will finish in two hours. Make it possible in advance: versioned artifacts, backward-compatible migrations, feature flags ([Practical Design Problems, Lesson 07](/design-problems/lesson-7)).

### 4. Investigate with a method

Use the loop from [Lesson 00](/debugging/lesson-0): measure, isolate, hypothesize, verify. Start with **"what changed?"**: deploys, config and feature-flag changes, infrastructure changes, traffic patterns, dependency or certificate expiry, a cron job, data growth. Correlate the **time the symptom began** with the change log.

Look in this order: **dashboards** (RED metrics: rate, errors, duration), **recent deploys**, **error tracking** (new exception types), **logs by request ID**, **traces** (where time is spent), **dependency health** (database, cache, queue, third parties), **saturation** (CPU, memory, connections, event-loop lag, disk).

### 5. Communicate

- **Internal:** short, regular, factual updates even when there is nothing new ("Still investigating, rollback in progress, next update in 15 minutes").
- **External:** a status page entry in plain language: what users see, what you are doing, when the next update is. No blame, no speculation, no internal jargon.
- Keep a **single source of truth** so people do not interrupt responders for status.

### 6. Resolve and verify

Confirm the fix in **metrics** (error rate and latency back to baseline), not just "it seems fine." Keep the heightened monitoring on for a while. Close the incident formally.

### 7. Blameless postmortem

Within a few days, write up:

- **Impact** (duration, users, money), **timeline**, **root cause(s)** (use "five whys", and prefer system causes to "human error"), **what went well / badly / luck**.
- **Action items** with owners and dates, in three categories: **prevent** (the bug class), **detect** (alert earlier), **mitigate** (faster rollback or smaller blast radius).
- **Blameless** means asking "how did our system allow this?" instead of "who did this?". People who fear blame hide information, and the next incident is worse.

## Scenarios

### 1. "An endpoint's latency doubled overnight."

**Symptom:** p95 for `GET /orders` went from 200 ms to 450 ms around 02:00; no alerts fired because the threshold was 1 s.

**Diagnose:**
1. **What changed at 02:00?** Check deploys, config, feature flags, **scheduled jobs** (backups, reports, cache warmers, `VACUUM`/ETL), traffic and data growth.
2. Split time with a **trace**: app handler, database, external calls, serialisation ([Lesson 02](/debugging/lesson-2)).
3. If it is the database: look at **slow query logs** and `pg_stat_statements`, then `EXPLAIN (ANALYZE, BUFFERS)` on the top offenders. Common nightly causes: a **batch job competing for I/O or locks**, **autovacuum**, a **statistics change that flips a plan**, an **index no longer used**, table growth past a threshold (cache no longer fits in memory).
4. Compare slow versus fast requests (large versus small accounts, particular tenants).

**Fix:** stop or reschedule the competing job, add or restore the index, refresh statistics, or add caching for the hot path.

**Prevent:** alert on **p95 relative to baseline** (not only an absolute limit), tag deploys on dashboards, run batch jobs on replicas or off-peak with resource limits, add query-plan checks to review.

### 2. "A bad deploy: error rate jumped from 0.1% to 12% five minutes after release."

**Diagnose:** the timing points at the release. Check error tracking for the new exception, compare versions in logs (version tag on every line).

**Fix:** **roll back immediately** (or disable the feature flag), confirm metrics recover, then reproduce in staging and fix forward.

**Prevent:** canary or **progressive rollout** (1% then 10% then 100%) with automatic rollback on error-rate thresholds, a smoke test after deploy, backward-compatible migrations so rollback is safe, and a feature flag around risky changes.

### 3. "Traffic suddenly increased 10x."

**First, is it good traffic?** A marketing success, a viral post, a bot or attack, a retry storm from your own clients, or a loop in a mobile app release. The response differs.

**Diagnose:** look at **who** (IP ranges, user agents, API keys), **what** (which endpoints), **when it began**. Check autoscaling limits, database connections, cache hit ratio, queue depth.

**Mitigate in order:** (1) **cache and serve static or stale content** (CDN, `stale-while-revalidate`), (2) **scale out** stateless tiers (and raise connection pooler limits so you do not just move the bottleneck to the database), (3) **rate-limit and shed load** (drop low-priority traffic, queue non-urgent work, return `429` with `Retry-After`) ([Practical Design Problems, Lesson 02](/design-problems/lesson-2)), (4) **degrade gracefully** (turn off recommendations, search suggestions, heavy reports), (5) **block abuse** at the edge or WAF.

**Prevent:** load tests at 5 to 10x, autoscaling policies and headroom, a **degradation plan** (which features can be switched off, in what order), CDN caching for public pages, capacity limits per tenant.

### 4. "The database fails over and the application stays down."

**Symptom:** the primary crashed and the replica was promoted, but the app throws connection errors for 20 minutes.

**Diagnose:** the app is still using the **old IP or cached DNS** for the database, **connection pools hold dead connections**, DNS TTL is high, or the app does not retry. Check whether the cluster endpoint changed.

**Fix:** restart or recycle pools, flush DNS caches, point to the new endpoint.

**Prevent:** use the provider's **cluster endpoint** (it follows the primary), set pool settings (`maxLifetime`, connection validation, short idle timeouts), **retry transient errors** with backoff, and **rehearse failovers** (game days).

### 5. "Everything slows down in a cascade; one slow dependency is taking the whole site down."

**Diagnose:** a downstream service became slow, request threads and connections pile up waiting on it, pools are exhausted, then unrelated endpoints fail too (a **cascading failure**). Retries from clients multiply the load (a **retry storm**).

**Fix:** open the **circuit breaker** or disable the dependent feature via flag, **time out** the calls aggressively, shed load, scale the dependency if it is capacity.

**Prevent:** **timeouts everywhere**, **circuit breakers**, **bulkheads** (separate pools per dependency), **retry budgets** with jitter, fallbacks, load shedding, and a dependency map ([Practical Design Problems, Lesson 07](/design-problems/lesson-7)).

### 6. "A queue is backing up and consumers keep crashing."

**Diagnose:** a **poison message** (always fails, is retried forever and blocks others), a consumer bug after a deploy, slow downstream processing, or a producer burst. Check the age of the oldest message, consumer error logs and the dead-letter queue.

**Fix:** move the poison message to the **DLQ**, roll back or fix the consumer, **scale consumers** if throughput is the problem, then **replay** the DLQ after the fix.

**Prevent:** max retry counts and a DLQ with alerts, **idempotent consumers**, queue-age (not just depth) alerts, schema versioning for messages.

### 7. "We discover a vulnerability in production (or a secret was leaked)."

**Respond in this order:**
1. **Contain**: disable or restrict the affected feature, **rotate** the leaked secret or key immediately, revoke sessions or tokens if needed, block the attack path (WAF rule).
2. **Assess**: what was exposed, since when, was it exploited (**audit logs**, access logs, anomaly search)? Preserve evidence.
3. **Fix** the root cause (patch, validation, authorization check) and deploy.
4. **Notify**: security team and leadership first; users and regulators if personal data was involved (laws set deadlines, for example 72 hours under GDPR).
5. **Postmortem** and prevention.

**Prevent:** secret scanning in CI, short-lived credentials, least privilege, dependency scanning, penetration tests, a **documented incident-response process** and a security contact. Never "fix quietly" without investigating exposure.

### 8. "Customers report wrong or duplicated data."

**Diagnose:** a **data-corruption** incident outranks most outages. Stop further damage first (pause the writer, disable the feature). Identify the **blast radius** with queries (which rows, which time window, which code path). Find the cause: a race condition, a non-idempotent retry, a bad migration, a faulty backfill.

**Fix:** correct the data with a **reviewed, reversible script** run on a copy first; keep **backups and point-in-time recovery** available; reconcile against a trusted source (payments provider, event log).

**Prevent:** constraints and idempotency ([Backend, Lesson 07](/backend/lesson-7)), migration rehearsals on production-sized copies, reconciliation jobs, audit trails.

### 9. "A certificate or domain expired and half the clients cannot connect."

**Fix first:** renew and deploy the certificate; intermediate certificate problems affect only some clients (older devices), which explains "half".

**Prevent:** automated renewal (ACME or managed certificates), **expiry monitoring and alerts** at 30, 14 and 7 days, an inventory of certificates, domains and API keys with owners.

### 10. "The third-party payment provider is down."

**Do:** switch the checkout to a **fallback** (a second provider, or "pay later"/queue the order), show honest messaging, **queue and retry** with idempotency keys so no one is charged twice, pause marketing spend, watch the provider's status page, reconcile when it returns.

**Prevent:** an abstraction over providers, circuit breakers and graceful degradation, idempotent payment flows, and a runbook.

### 11. "You are on call at 3 a.m. and the page says 'high error rate'. What do you do in the first 10 minutes?"

1. **Acknowledge** the page and **check impact** (is it real? dashboards, a synthetic check).
2. **What changed?** Last deploy and flags; roll back if correlated.
3. **Scope it:** all users or some region/tenant/endpoint? Which dependency is red?
4. **Mitigate**: roll back, restart a wedged instance, scale, fail over, flag off.
5. **Escalate** early if it is bigger than you or outside your area; waking a colleague is not a failure.
6. **Communicate** in the channel and the status page.
7. **Write down** what you did for the postmortem.

## How to answer incident questions in an interview

Structure your answer as **Detect, Mitigate, Diagnose, Communicate, Resolve, Learn**, and mention these senior signals: you **roll back first**, you **look at what changed**, you **scope impact before diving deep**, you **communicate**, and you run a **blameless postmortem** with prevent, detect and mitigate action items. If asked "tell me about a real incident", use the **STAR** format with specifics: the symptom, your role, the decision you made and why, the outcome and what changed afterwards.

## Common mistakes

1. **Debugging before mitigating**, so users suffer while you investigate.
2. **No clear incident commander**, causing duplicated or conflicting actions.
3. **Changing several things at once**, so you cannot tell what worked.
4. **Silence** towards stakeholders and customers.
5. **Blaming people** instead of fixing systems.
6. **Declaring victory without checking metrics.**
7. **No follow-through** on postmortem action items.

## Interview questions

### Q1. Walk me through how you handle a production incident.

Declare it and assign roles, assess impact and severity, mitigate first (roll back, flag off, scale, shed load, fail over), then investigate with a method starting from "what changed?", communicate regularly internally and externally, verify recovery in metrics, and finish with a blameless postmortem whose action items prevent, detect and mitigate that class of failure.

### Q2. Scenario: an endpoint's latency doubled overnight. How do you investigate?

I check what changed around the time it started (deploys, config, scheduled jobs, traffic, data growth), split the request time with traces into app, database and external calls, and inspect the slow-query logs and query plans if the database is involved. I compare fast and slow requests, fix the cause (reschedule a job, index, statistics, cache), and add baseline-relative latency alerts.

### Q3. Scenario: traffic suddenly increases 10x. What do you do?

First establish whether it is legitimate or abusive and where it is concentrated. Then mitigate by serving cached content, scaling stateless tiers while protecting the database (pooler, limits), rate-limiting and shedding low-priority load, degrading non-essential features and blocking abuse at the edge. Afterwards load test and document the degradation plan.

### Q4. Scenario: you discover a vulnerability in production. What is your process?

Contain it (disable the feature, rotate secrets, revoke sessions, add a block), assess exposure with logs while preserving evidence, fix the root cause, notify the right people including users and regulators if personal data is involved, and run a postmortem. I never fix quietly without checking whether it was exploited.

### Q5. Why roll back before diagnosing?

Because the goal is to restore service quickly and a rollback is fast, verifiable and usually correct when symptoms start right after a release. It removes pressure so you can investigate calmly, whereas debugging forward leaves users affected for longer.

### Q6. What makes a postmortem blameless and useful?

It focuses on system and process causes ("how did our safeguards allow this?") rather than individuals, includes a timeline, impact, root causes and what went well or badly, and ends with owned, dated action items across prevention, detection and mitigation. Blamelessness keeps people honest, which is what makes the next incident shorter.

### Q7. How do you prevent cascading failures?

Timeouts on every outbound call, circuit breakers, bulkheads that isolate resource pools per dependency, retry budgets with backoff and jitter, load shedding, graceful degradation and capacity limits, plus testing these behaviours with game days or fault injection.

## Key takeaways

- **Mitigate first** (roll back, flag off, scale, shed, fail over); investigate second.
- Always ask **"what changed?"** and scope impact before deep-diving.
- Use **roles**, a **timeline**, and **regular communication** (internal and external).
- Verify recovery in **metrics**; then run a **blameless postmortem** with prevent, detect and mitigate actions.
- Build for incidents in advance: **rollbacks, flags, canaries, timeouts, circuit breakers, DLQs, runbooks, alerts relative to baseline**.

> **In one line:** at senior level the hardest questions are not about code but about **decisions**: when to split a system, how to change a database without downtime, how to say no to a deadline. Answer with a **framework** (options, trade-offs, a recommendation, a rollback), not an opinion.

Fourteen scenarios on architecture, data and working with people. Interviewers use them to see whether you will make good calls when nobody tells you the answer.

## A decision framework you can reuse

1. **Clarify the goal and constraints** (what problem are we solving, for whom, by when, with what team?).
2. **List 2 to 3 options**, including "do nothing" and "the smallest change".
3. **Compare on what matters**: risk, cost, time to value, reversibility, operational burden, team skills.
4. **Recommend**, and say what **evidence** would change your mind.
5. **Plan the rollout**: incremental steps, **how to roll back**, how to measure success.
6. **Communicate** to stakeholders in terms of outcomes and risks.

## The scenarios

### 1. "Deploys of our monolith are slow and risky. Should we move to microservices?"

**Diagnose the real problem first.** Slow, risky deploys can come from **missing tests, a slow pipeline, tight coupling or no feature flags**, none of which microservices fix by themselves. Microservices bring **network failures, distributed data, versioned contracts, observability needs and much more operational work**.

**Options:** (a) improve the monolith (faster CI, tests, flags, canary deploys); (b) a **modular monolith** with enforced boundaries; (c) extract **one or two services** where there is a genuine need (a different scaling profile, a separate team, isolation of a risky component); (d) full microservices.

**Recommendation:** start with (a) and (b); extract a service only with a clear reason, behind a stable interface, using the **strangler** approach. Measure deploy frequency, lead time and change-failure rate before and after.

### 2. "The legacy system is a mess. Rewrite it or refactor?"

**Default to incremental.** A rewrite delivers no value until the end, is hard to estimate, and tends to recreate old bugs while the old system keeps changing (the "second-system" trap).

**Strangler fig pattern:** put a **facade or router** in front; build new functionality (or re-implement one slice) in the new system; **route traffic gradually** (by feature, route, tenant or percentage); keep both running; retire pieces of the old system as they become unused. Add **characterisation tests** around legacy behaviour first.

**When a rewrite is justified:** the platform is end-of-life with no migration path, the domain is small and well understood, or the cost of keeping it exceeds a full rebuild. Even then, ship in vertical slices.

### 3. "Pages are slow, so the team wants to add Redis caching everywhere."

**Ask first:** *why* is it slow? Measure ([Lesson 00](/debugging/lesson-0)). Caching hides a slow query and adds **invalidation** and **consistency** problems.

**If caching is justified:** pick **what** (read-heavy, expensive, tolerant of staleness), **where** (CDN, Redis, in-process), the **key design**, the **TTL and invalidation** strategy (explicit on write, versioned keys), **stampede protection**, and **what must never be cached** (personalised or strongly consistent data). Add a metric for **hit ratio** and a way to bypass the cache for debugging. ([Lesson 02](/debugging/lesson-2))

### 4. "We need to rename a column (or change its type) on a 200 GB table with zero downtime."

Use **expand and contract** (parallel change): never make a change that old and new code cannot both survive.

1. **Expand:** add the new column (nullable, no heavy default), **without** removing the old one.
2. **Dual-write:** deploy code that writes **both** columns (or use a trigger).
3. **Backfill** existing rows in small **batches** with throttling, idempotent and resumable.
4. **Verify** the two columns match (a reconciliation query, row counts, checksums).
5. **Switch reads** to the new column behind a **feature flag**; watch metrics; keep the ability to flip back.
6. **Contract:** stop writing the old column, then **drop it in a later release** after a safe window.

**Safety rules:** each step is a separate deploy and is **reversible**; use online DDL (`CREATE INDEX CONCURRENTLY`, `NOT VALID` constraints then `VALIDATE`), set **lock timeouts**, rehearse on a production-sized copy, and schedule off-peak. The same pattern handles table splits, type changes and service-to-service data moves. ([Lesson 11, scenario 11](/debugging/lesson-11))

### 5. "Backfill 100 million rows without hurting production."

**Approach:** run it as a **throttled, resumable background job**: process in batches by primary-key ranges (not `OFFSET`), commit each batch, **sleep or adapt** based on database load and replica lag, make each batch **idempotent**, record progress (a cursor) so a restart continues, run on a replica or off-peak where possible, and **monitor** (lock waits, replication lag, error rate). Add a **kill switch**. Verify results with sampling and counts before cutting over.

### 6. "Reads are overwhelming the primary database. How do you scale?"

**In order:** (1) **fix the queries and indexes** and add caching for hot reads; (2) **read replicas** for read-heavy endpoints; (3) **partition** big tables or archive cold data; (4) **shard** only when a single primary cannot handle writes or size.

**Replicas bring replication lag:** route only **lag-tolerant** reads to replicas, send **read-your-own-writes** to the primary (or use a session/LSN-based consistency token), and monitor lag with alerts. ([Backend, Lesson 07](/backend/lesson-7))

### 7. "We are multi-tenant. How do we make sure one customer never sees another's data?"

**Layers of defence:** (1) a **tenant ID on every row** and every query (enforced centrally, not by convention); (2) **database row-level security** (PostgreSQL RLS) as a safety net; (3) tenant context derived from the **verified session**, never from the request body; (4) **per-tenant caches and keys** (`tenant:{id}:…`), per-tenant encryption keys for sensitive data; (5) **tests** that attempt cross-tenant access on every endpoint; (6) audit logging and anomaly alerts; (7) consider **schema-per-tenant or database-per-tenant** for strong isolation or noisy-neighbour control, at higher operational cost.

**Also:** per-tenant rate limits and quotas so one customer cannot starve others.

### 8. "The team has a lot of tech debt. How do you prioritise it and convince the business?"

**Make it visible and tie it to outcomes.** List debt items with **impact** (incident count, slow delivery, onboarding time, security risk) and **cost to fix**. Prioritise by **interest rate**: debt in the code you change every week costs more than debt in a stable corner. Express the case in **business terms** ("this module causes 40% of our bugs and adds two days to every change; fixing it saves about X per quarter").

**How to schedule it:** a standing allocation (say 15 to 20% of capacity), **boy-scout fixes** alongside feature work, and a few **scoped refactoring projects** with clear exit criteria. Avoid "big-bang" cleanups that deliver nothing visible.

### 9. "A stakeholder demands a date that you believe is unrealistic."

**Do:** understand **why** the date matters; decompose the work and estimate with **ranges and assumptions**; identify the **critical path and risks**; then present **options** instead of just "no": reduce scope (an MVP), move the date, add people (with the cost of onboarding), or accept risk with a mitigation plan. Agree on **what will be cut first** if it slips, and re-estimate as you learn. Keep a record of the trade-off decision.

**Avoid:** silently agreeing and burning out the team, or padding without explaining.

### 10. "In code review, a teammate and you disagree on an approach."

**Aim for the best outcome, not winning.** Make sure you **understand their reasoning**; separate **preference** from **correctness or risk**; use **data** (benchmarks, a small prototype) or the team's **standards and ADRs**; propose a compromise or an experiment; if it is still stuck, **timebox** and involve a tech lead or decide by the agreed principle (reversibility, consistency with existing code). **Disagree and commit** once decided, and write down the decision. Keep comments about the code, kind and specific.

### 11. "Should we build our own auth/search/billing or buy a service?"

**Compare** on: **strategic differentiation** (is this core to our product?), **total cost** (build, run, maintain, security, compliance), **time to market**, **fit and flexibility**, **lock-in and exit cost**, **reliability and support**, **data residency and compliance**. **Default:** buy or use open source for **commodity** capabilities (auth, email, payments, search) and build what **differentiates** you. Reduce lock-in with an **abstraction layer** and clear data export. Run a **time-boxed proof of concept** with real requirements and edge cases before committing.

### 12. "We have almost no monitoring. Where do you start?"

**Order of value:** (1) **uptime and error-rate checks** with alerts on **user-visible symptoms** (synthetic checks, 5xx rate, latency percentiles); (2) **structured logs with request IDs** and error tracking; (3) the **RED metrics** per service and key business metrics (orders, signups); (4) **resource metrics** (CPU, memory, DB connections, queue depth, event-loop lag); (5) **distributed tracing**; (6) **SLOs** and error budgets; (7) runbooks linked from alerts and an on-call rotation. Start small: five good alerts beat fifty noisy ones. ([Backend, Lesson 05](/backend/lesson-5))

### 13. "Our CI takes 40 minutes and developers stopped waiting for it."

**Measure** where the time goes (a breakdown per job and step), then attack the biggest parts: **cache dependencies and build outputs**, **parallelise** jobs and **shard tests**, run only **affected** tests and builds (monorepo tooling such as Nx or Turborepo with remote caching), move slow end-to-end tests to a **post-merge** or nightly stage while keeping a fast **pre-merge smoke** set, use **bigger or faster runners**, fix and quarantine flaky tests, and keep Docker layers cacheable. Set a **target** (for example under 10 minutes) and track it.

### 14. "A bug that you introduced reached production and cost the company money."

**Own it, fix it, learn from it.** Contain first (roll back or flag off), communicate clearly and promptly (no excuses), fix the data if affected, and take part in a **blameless postmortem** that identifies **why the system allowed it** (a missing test, an unsafe default, no canary) and adds a **guard**. In an interview, show **accountability and growth**: what happened, what you did in the first hour, what changed afterwards. Avoid blaming others or minimising impact. ([Lesson 10](/debugging/lesson-10))

## Interview questions

### Q1. Scenario: the monolith deploys are slow and risky. Do you move to microservices?

Not by default. I first diagnose the cause (missing tests, slow CI, coupling, no flags), then improve the monolith and enforce module boundaries, and extract a service only for a concrete reason (different scaling, team ownership, isolation) behind a stable interface using the strangler approach, measuring deploy frequency and failure rate before and after.

### Q2. Scenario: rename a column on a huge table with zero downtime. How?

Expand and contract: add the new column, dual-write, backfill in throttled batches, verify equality, switch reads behind a flag, stop writing the old column and drop it in a later release. Each step is a separate, reversible deploy, uses online DDL and lock timeouts and is rehearsed on a production-sized copy.

### Q3. Scenario: rewrite or refactor the legacy system?

Prefer incremental replacement with the strangler fig pattern: put a facade in front, characterise existing behaviour with tests, migrate slices and route traffic gradually, retire old parts when unused. Consider a full rewrite only when the platform is end-of-life or the domain is small and well understood, and still deliver in vertical slices.

### Q4. Scenario: a stakeholder demands an unrealistic deadline. What do you do?

Understand why the date matters, break down and estimate with ranges and risks, and present options: reduce scope, move the date, add resources with their cost or accept a specific risk with mitigation. Agree what is cut first if it slips and re-estimate as we learn.

### Q5. Scenario: how do you prioritise tech debt?

Quantify impact (incidents, delivery speed, security) and cost, prioritise debt in frequently changed areas, express it in business terms, reserve a standing share of capacity and use scoped projects with exit criteria rather than big-bang cleanups.

### Q6. Scenario: how do you guarantee tenant isolation in a multi-tenant system?

Enforce tenant IDs centrally in every query, add database row-level security as a safety net, derive tenant from the verified session, separate caches and keys per tenant, test cross-tenant access for every endpoint, audit and alert, and consider schema or database per tenant where stronger isolation justifies the cost.

### Q7. Scenario: you disagree with a teammate on an approach in review. How do you resolve it?

Understand their reasoning, separate preference from correctness, use data or a small prototype and the team's standards, propose a compromise, timebox the discussion, escalate to a tech lead if needed, then disagree and commit and record the decision.

### Q8. Scenario: you shipped a bug that cost money. How do you talk about it?

Describe the impact honestly, the immediate containment, how you communicated, the blameless analysis of why the system let it through, and the concrete guard you added. Show accountability and learning rather than excuses.

## Key takeaways

- Use a **framework**: clarify, options, trade-offs, recommendation, rollout and rollback, communication.
- **Incremental beats big-bang**: strangler fig, expand and contract, throttled backfills, modular monolith first.
- **Measure before you change** (caching, scaling, CI) and **tie decisions to outcomes**.
- Soft-skill scenarios are tested too: **negotiate scope with options, disagree and commit, own your mistakes**.
